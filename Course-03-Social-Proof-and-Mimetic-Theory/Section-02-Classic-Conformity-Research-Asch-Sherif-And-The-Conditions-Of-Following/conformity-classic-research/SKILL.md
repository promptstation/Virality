# Classic Conformity Research: Asch, Sherif, and the Conditions of Following: Professional Module Builder

## Mission

Produce professional-level material on what the classic conformity experiments establish about the conditions of social influence — and how to read them as design parameters. The unit of work is a defensible artifact — a learning module, an evidence brief, a case diagnosis, or a strategy memo — in which every claim is tied to a source, a mechanism, and a boundary condition.

Optimize simultaneously for:

- empirical precision (what the canonical studies and works actually found)
- conceptual clarity (the core terms kept strictly distinct)
- causal discipline (correlational and causal claims never blurred)
- practical utility (a practitioner can act on the output the same day)
- intellectual honesty (limits and boundary conditions as visible as the findings)

Do not produce generic advice or pop-science hype. The register is that of an advanced professional training program: confident where the evidence is strong, explicitly conditional where it is not.

## Use Cases

### New professional learning module
When asked to create a full module, course unit, or workshop on this topic:
1. Establish scope: audience, depth, and which claims the module must cover.
2. Build the evidence base from primary sources and canonical works.
3. Develop the core mechanisms and diagnostic logic.
4. Add method, critique, and application sections.
5. End with exercises and the delivery checklists.
6. Deliver the complete artifact with no placeholders.

### Brief or leadership memo
When asked for a short document that must decide something:
1. State the question the document must answer in one sentence.
2. Include only findings strong enough to bear that decision, each with its mechanism and source.
3. Convert results into plain-language implications.
4. End with the specific decisions the evidence changes.
5. Cap length; cut context before cutting caveats.

### Case diagnosis
When asked to explain why a specific piece of content, campaign, or product spread (or did not):
1. Identify the operative mechanisms — never a single assumed cause.
2. Map the case onto the diagnostic framework of this skill.
3. Run the differential: which mechanisms were active, which alternatives were considered and set aside.
4. State confidence and the evidence that would revise the diagnosis.

### Strategy design
When asked to design a campaign, content system, or program around this lever:
1. Fix the objective, audience, and platform context.
2. Select the mechanisms from this skill that fit that context.
3. Specify implementation, sequencing, and the measurement plan.
4. State the boundary conditions under which the plan breaks.

## Core Output Requirements

Regardless of format:

- Every named finding or claim carries its source or mechanism in the same breath.
- Correlational and causal claims are labeled as such wherever they matter.
- Effect claims carry their metric and a plain-language rendering.
- Boundary conditions are stated as explicit “applies when / breaks down when” conditions.
- Examples use real, named studies, campaigns, or artifacts — not invented case studies.
- No padding sections, generic intro/outro filler, or advice that would read the same for any topic.

## Phase 1 — Scope and Claim Audit

### Artifact and audience
What the user actually needs (module vs. memo vs. diagnosis) and who reads it. Different readers make different cuts of the same material.

### Claim inventory
List the claims the artifact will make. For each, note the source or mechanism that supports it and whether that support is correlational, experimental, or canonical. Downgrade or cut unsupported claims — not the caveat.

### Depth budget
Match length to the decision the artifact serves. The fix for a weak document is choosing stronger claims to carry it, not adding hedges to weaker ones.

## Phase 2 — Foundations and Core Content

1. Reconstruct Sherif's autokinetic study: under true ambiguity, groups form shared norms, and the norm persists after individuals leave — the origin of group standards.
2. Reconstruct Asch's line studies: with a visible majority giving a wrong answer, a substantial minority conforms publicly, and private judgment tracks the public response less than intuition assumes.
3. Extract the conditions: ambiguity is the engine (conformity collapses when the standard is objective and clear), unanimity is the switch (a single dissenter breaks the majority's power), and group size saturates (three to four conformers carry most of the effect).
4. Teach the commitment and identity amplifiers: public commitment and in-group identity raise conformity, and the private-public gap is the compliance-versus-acceptance distinction.
5. Introduce minority influence: a consistent, confident minority can shift the group's norm over time — the experimental root of trend formation and opinion leadership.

## Phase 3 — Method and Technique

- Scoring a decision context for ambiguity (is there an objective standard?)
- Auditing the visible majority: size, unanimity, and the presence of a dissenter
- Designing a conformity experiment: majority condition, dissenter condition, public/private measures
- Measuring the private-public gap (compliance versus acceptance)
- Estimating the group-size saturation point for a specific population

## Phase 4 — Analysis and Diagnosis

- Classify the target decision as ambiguous or objective — proof only carries weight where ambiguity is real.
- Find the dissenter: a single credible ally in the visible environment breaks a hostile majority.
- Detect the compliance-without-acceptance pattern: public conformity with private resistance, and the backlash it stores.
- Score the minority condition: is the target audience the majority or the minority in the visible environment, and what does that imply?

## Phase 5 — Application and Design

- Create objective standards where possible — a clear standard is the strongest conformity breaker and the strongest trust builder.
- Place a single credible ally in the visible environment before scaling the majority message.
- Use the saturation point: three or four visible adopters are enough; a hundred adds nothing.
- For trend work, seed a consistent minority — consistency is the minority's only capital.

## Phase 6 — Ethics and Boundary Conditions

- Manufactured majorities (bots, paid crowds) exploit the conformity mechanism; the discovery cost is a trust collapse that outlives the campaign.
- Conformity pressure on a vulnerable minority (making the deviant the visible target) is coercion, not proof.
- The private-public gap matters ethically: compliance extracted under unanimity pressure is not consent.

## Phase 7 — Exercises and Assessment

For learning artifacts, include exercises that force the diagnosis skill, not recall. Use the standard set:

1. Score a target decision for ambiguity, majority size, unanimity, and ally presence; predict the conformity level with the paradigm's numbers.
2. Design the two-condition experiment (unanimous majority vs. majority + one dissenter) with the public and private measures.
3. Take a real adoption case and diagnose it: which conditions were present, which were missing, and what the data should show.
4. Estimate the saturation point: from a dataset of adoption counts, show where adding more visible adopters stops adding effect.
5. Design a minority-influence seeding plan: the consistent message, the credible seed, and the time horizon the paradigm implies.
6. Write the ethics review for a visible-majority campaign: authenticity, coercion check, and the vulnerable-minority check.

For each exercise provide scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

## Phase 8 — Quality Gate and Delivery

Before delivering, run the delivery checklists. The artifact fails the gate on any “no.”

### Context scoring
- Ambiguity is scored
- Majority size and unanimity are counted
- The ally position is identified

### Experiment design
- The conditions are specified
- Public and private measures are separate
- The saturation check is included

### Diagnosis
- The conditions map to the observed behavior
- The compliance-acceptance gap is measured
- The minority position is scored

### Delivery gate
- The ethics checks were run
- The breaker (ally or standard) is in the design
- The post-period norm measurement is scheduled

## Anti-Patterns

conformity predicted for objective decisions; the unanimous manufactured majority; the ally omitted by assumption; compliance read as acceptance; the saturation point ignored; the minority seeded inconsistently.

## Decision Heuristic

Before any claim or recommendation, ask:

1. Is there an objective standard here, or is this genuinely ambiguous?
2. How big is the visible majority, is it unanimous, and where is the dissenter?
3. Is the response compliance or acceptance, and which does the goal require?
4. Are we the majority or the minority in this environment, and what does that strategy imply?
5. Would this design survive a replication and an ethics review?

If a claim cannot survive all of these questions, it is not in the artifact.

## Final Principle

The goal is not a bigger crowd. It is a design that knows exactly which conditions make influence work — and which break it — and uses the breakers as deliberately as the levers.
