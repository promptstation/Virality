# Course 03 — Social Proof and Mimetic Theory (Researched Curriculum Outline)

Source: `uploads/Virality.txt`, entry 3. Section count: 8 (compressed to ≤15). Progression: beginner → expert.

**Social Proof Fundamentals: When Others Are the Message**

**Summary:** The ground floor: social proof — using observed behavior as evidence of what to do, especially under uncertainty. The section defines descriptive norms (what people do) versus injunctive norms (what people approve of), the power of numbers and similarity in making proof credible, the conditions under which social proof works (uncertainty, salience, relevance), and the classic failure modes (too few examples, the wrong reference group, proof that backfires). It anchors in Cialdini's social-proof framework and the hotel-towel and energy-saving-letter field experiments.

**Absorbed Skill:** Identify and apply social-proof mechanisms — descriptive and injunctive norms — in communication and product design; Diagnose social-proof failure: wrong reference, too few, or salient counter-proof.

**Classic Conformity Research: Asch, Sherif, and the Conditions of Following**

**Summary:** The experimental foundation. The section reconstructs Sherif's autokinetic experiment (norm formation under true ambiguity) and Asch's line-judgment studies (conformity to a visible majority even when the answer is obvious), extracts the conditions — ambiguity, group size, unanimity, commitment, and the presence of a single ally — and translates them into practical predictions about when social proof will and will not move a target audience, including the minority-influence pattern that underpins trend formation.

**Absorbed Skill:** Interpret the classic conformity paradigms and extract their operational conditions (ambiguity, unanimity, group size, ally); Predict from the paradigms when social proof will move a specific audience and when it will fail.

**Mimetic Desire: Girard and the Imitation Engine**

**Summary:** The theoretical pivot from proof to imitation. The section introduces René Girard's mimetic theory: desire is not innate to the object but learned from models — the triangular structure of desire (subject, model, object) — and how mimesis generates convergence, rivalry, and the scapegoat mechanism. It covers mimetic contagion (how a desire, once modeled, spreads and multiplies), the difference between mimetic and autonomous desire, and why this matters for trends, fashion, and viral content: we do not want things, we want what the model wants.

**Absorbed Skill:** Map any desire or trend onto the mimetic triangle (subject, model, object) and identify the model doing the work; Distinguish mimetic from autonomous desire and predict convergence and rivalry dynamics.

**Mimesis in Consumption and Trends**

**Summary:** Mimesis at market scale. The section applies the triangle to consumption and trend formation: fashion cycles (the innovation–diffusion–saturation–exhaustion rhythm driven by model changes), status goods and conspicuous consumption (Veblen), aspirational modeling in advertising (who the ad casts as the model), bandwagon and snob effects, and the modern meme-fied trend cycle on social platforms where the model chain is visible in real time. It closes with how to read a trend's phase and its model chain to predict its trajectory.

**Absorbed Skill:** Analyze a trend's phase and model chain to predict its trajectory and saturation; Design a consumption strategy that harnesses mimetic dynamics — modeling, aspiration, bandwagon, and distinction.

**Measuring Social Proof and Imitation**

**Summary:** The measurement layer. The section covers how to measure proof and imitation empirically: adoption-rate experiments (with and without the proof cue), conformity-rate measures from the paradigms, observational and network measures of imitation (who copied whom, with what lag), platform metrics (likes, shares, follower growth as visible proof), and the field-experiment designs (A/B norm messages, seed-versus-control) that establish causality. It covers the pitfalls: attribution (which proof did the work?), contamination (the control sees the proof), and the lag structure of imitation.

**Absorbed Skill:** Design experiments and observational studies that measure the causal effect of social proof and imitation; Analyze imitation data — who copied whom, with what lag — to identify the model chains in a market.

**Applying Social Proof to Viral Communication**

**Summary:** The application layer: proof as a communication and platform mechanic. The section covers the platform-native proof instruments — likes, shares, follower counts, live activity, comments, 'people like you' framing — how to design for them (proof placement, proof timing, proof format), the proof architecture of a viral asset (the visible evidence that rides with the content), and the cross-course integration with K-factor (the proof that converts the invited) and recommendation engines (the engagement counts the ranking system reads). It closes with the proof content calendar: which proof to build, which to borrow, and which to never fake.

**Absorbed Skill:** Design platform-native social-proof elements into content and campaigns — placement, timing, format — for maximum transmission effect; Integrate proof with referral loops and algorithmic signals so the visible adoption does the converting.

**Ethics and Dark Patterns of Proof and Imitation**

**Summary:** The discipline layer. The section examines the manipulation modes of proof and imitation: manufactured crowds and astroturfing, fake scarcity and staged urgency, herd engineering (manufacturing the bandwagon), FOMO design, shaming the laggard, and the platform incentives that reward manufactured proof. It covers the evidence on trust collapse after discovery, the regulatory and platform-policy landscape, and the professional ethics framework: authenticity, disclosure, non-coercion, and the vulnerable-audience check — with the redesign of each dark pattern into its honest equivalent.

**Absorbed Skill:** Evaluate a proof or imitation tactic against a professional ethics framework and identify the manipulation mode; Redesign a dark pattern (manufactured crowd, FOMO, shaming) into its honest equivalent without losing the effect.

**Synthesis: Proof, Imitation, and the Diffusion System**

**Summary:** The capstone: proof and mimesis as a system. The section integrates the course: the conformity conditions (when proof works), the mimetic structure (what is being imitated and by whom), the measurement (the design that proves it), and the ethics (the line) — into a single operating model. It covers the proof-imitation program: the baseline, the model chain, the platform instruments, the experiment, and the ethics review, running as a standing system; the cross-course integration with diffusion (the S-curve), K-factor (the loop), and network effects (the topology); and the synthesis case: a full adoption campaign, from baseline to model-chain design to measurement to ethics sign-off.

**Absorbed Skill:** Build and run a proof-imitation program: baseline, model chain, platform instruments, experiment, and ethics review as a standing system; Integrate proof and mimesis with diffusion, K-factor, and network concepts into a single adoption strategy.
