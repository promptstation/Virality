# Step 2 — Generated Prompt (Result of the Reusable Prompt)

```
Develop a comprehensive, professional-level learning module on **“Mimetic Desire: Girard and the Imitation Engine”** as part of an advanced course on how others' behavior — observed, measured, and imitated — drives adoption, sharing, and trend formation. The module should how desire is learned from models rather than generated by objects — the mimetic triangle — and what that implies for trends, competition, and viral demand.

The material should teach the mimetic structure: subject, model, object — and the dynamics (convergence, rivalry, contagion) that follow from learned desire. Go beyond the neoclassical assumption that preferences are stable, innate, and object-driven and examine Girard's argument, its anthropological support, and its economic and marketing applications.

Explain the triangular structure: desire flows subject → model → object; the object is desired because the model desires it, and the model's desire is what is actually imitated.

Teach mimetic versus autonomous desire: most consumption is mimetic (learned from a model — the category, the status, the lifestyle) while autonomous desire (a specific functional need) is the minority case.

Show the dynamics: mimetic convergence (subjects imitating the same model converge on the same object), mimetic rivalry (convergent subjects collide over the now-shared object), and the cultural resolutions (exclusion, distinction, scapegoating).

Teach mimetic contagion: a desire, once visible in a model, is itself copyable — the mechanism by which trends accelerate, with the model chain (who models whom) as the transmission route.

Introduce the distinction problem: as a mimetic good saturates, its desire value comes from scarcity of access or from the model's continued distance — the logic of status goods.

Include practical methods for working at a professional level:
* Mapping a demand case onto the triangle: naming the subject, the model(s), and the object
* Auditing a market for model chains (who aspires to whom)
* Distinguishing mimetic from autonomous demand in a demand breakdown
* Tracking convergence: measuring the overlap of the desired object across imitating subjects
* Detecting rivalry signals: price inflation, exclusivity theater, copycat wars

Include practical exercises that require the learner to:
1. Map a real demand case onto the triangle — subject, model, object — and identify the model chain with evidence.
2. Decompose a product's demand into autonomous and mimetic components, with the measurement plan for each.
3. Track a trend's convergence: measure the object overlap across imitating groups over time and plot the convergence curve.
4. Detect the rivalry phase in a market: the price, exclusivity, and copycat signals — and the phase's strategic implications.
5. Design the model-chain position for a brand: the choice (model / model's choice / aspirational object), the distance management, and the risk note.
6. Write the ethics review for a model-driven demand campaign: the authenticity of the model's use, the disclosure, and the fickle-demand risk management.

For each exercise, provide the scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

Structure the material progressively, beginning with the triangle and the mimetic/autonomous distinction, moving through convergence, rivalry, and contagion dynamics, then to a model-chain strategy with distance and ethics managed. Establish clear conceptual distinctions between **mimetic desire, autonomous desire, model, convergence, and rivalry** before showing how they interact.

Use professional terminology from social psychology, behavioral economics, and cultural theory where relevant, but explain specialized terminology in clear language. Do not make the material sound like generic AI-generated advice; the register should be that of an advanced professional training module used by behavioral researchers, brand strategists, and social scientists.

Where useful, distinguish established research findings from professional conventions and informed recommendations.

Pay particular attention to the relationship between this module and principles such as **the triangular structure, model chains, convergence-rivalry dynamics, and distance management**. Connect these concepts to concrete analytical and design decisions rather than discussing them only theoretically.

Create a professional framework that behavioral researchers, brand strategists, and social scientists could actually use in a real project.
```
