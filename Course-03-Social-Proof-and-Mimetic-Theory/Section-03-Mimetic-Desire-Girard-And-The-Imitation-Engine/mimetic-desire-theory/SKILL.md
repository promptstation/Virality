# Mimetic Desire: Girard and the Imitation Engine: Professional Module Builder

## Mission

Produce professional-level material on how desire is learned from models rather than generated by objects — the mimetic triangle — and what that implies for trends, competition, and viral demand. The unit of work is a defensible artifact — a learning module, an evidence brief, a case diagnosis, or a strategy memo — in which every claim is tied to a source, a mechanism, and a boundary condition.

Optimize simultaneously for:

- empirical precision (what the canonical studies and works actually found)
- conceptual clarity (the core terms kept strictly distinct)
- causal discipline (correlational and causal claims never blurred)
- practical utility (a practitioner can act on the output the same day)
- intellectual honesty (limits and boundary conditions as visible as the findings)

Do not produce generic advice or pop-science hype. The register is that of an advanced professional training program: confident where the evidence is strong, explicitly conditional where it is not.

## Use Cases

### New professional learning module
When asked to create a full module, course unit, or workshop on this topic:
1. Establish scope: audience, depth, and which claims the module must cover.
2. Build the evidence base from primary sources and canonical works.
3. Develop the core mechanisms and diagnostic logic.
4. Add method, critique, and application sections.
5. End with exercises and the delivery checklists.
6. Deliver the complete artifact with no placeholders.

### Brief or leadership memo
When asked for a short document that must decide something:
1. State the question the document must answer in one sentence.
2. Include only findings strong enough to bear that decision, each with its mechanism and source.
3. Convert results into plain-language implications.
4. End with the specific decisions the evidence changes.
5. Cap length; cut context before cutting caveats.

### Case diagnosis
When asked to explain why a specific piece of content, campaign, or product spread (or did not):
1. Identify the operative mechanisms — never a single assumed cause.
2. Map the case onto the diagnostic framework of this skill.
3. Run the differential: which mechanisms were active, which alternatives were considered and set aside.
4. State confidence and the evidence that would revise the diagnosis.

### Strategy design
When asked to design a campaign, content system, or program around this lever:
1. Fix the objective, audience, and platform context.
2. Select the mechanisms from this skill that fit that context.
3. Specify implementation, sequencing, and the measurement plan.
4. State the boundary conditions under which the plan breaks.

## Core Output Requirements

Regardless of format:

- Every named finding or claim carries its source or mechanism in the same breath.
- Correlational and causal claims are labeled as such wherever they matter.
- Effect claims carry their metric and a plain-language rendering.
- Boundary conditions are stated as explicit “applies when / breaks down when” conditions.
- Examples use real, named studies, campaigns, or artifacts — not invented case studies.
- No padding sections, generic intro/outro filler, or advice that would read the same for any topic.

## Phase 1 — Scope and Claim Audit

### Artifact and audience
What the user actually needs (module vs. memo vs. diagnosis) and who reads it. Different readers make different cuts of the same material.

### Claim inventory
List the claims the artifact will make. For each, note the source or mechanism that supports it and whether that support is correlational, experimental, or canonical. Downgrade or cut unsupported claims — not the caveat.

### Depth budget
Match length to the decision the artifact serves. The fix for a weak document is choosing stronger claims to carry it, not adding hedges to weaker ones.

## Phase 2 — Foundations and Core Content

1. Explain the triangular structure: desire flows subject → model → object; the object is desired because the model desires it, and the model's desire is what is actually imitated.
2. Teach mimetic versus autonomous desire: most consumption is mimetic (learned from a model — the category, the status, the lifestyle) while autonomous desire (a specific functional need) is the minority case.
3. Show the dynamics: mimetic convergence (subjects imitating the same model converge on the same object), mimetic rivalry (convergent subjects collide over the now-shared object), and the cultural resolutions (exclusion, distinction, scapegoating).
4. Teach mimetic contagion: a desire, once visible in a model, is itself copyable — the mechanism by which trends accelerate, with the model chain (who models whom) as the transmission route.
5. Introduce the distinction problem: as a mimetic good saturates, its desire value comes from scarcity of access or from the model's continued distance — the logic of status goods.

## Phase 3 — Method and Technique

- Mapping a demand case onto the triangle: naming the subject, the model(s), and the object
- Auditing a market for model chains (who aspires to whom)
- Distinguishing mimetic from autonomous demand in a demand breakdown
- Tracking convergence: measuring the overlap of the desired object across imitating subjects
- Detecting rivalry signals: price inflation, exclusivity theater, copycat wars

## Phase 4 — Analysis and Diagnosis

- Diagnose unexplained demand as mimetic: name the model, and the mystery becomes a mechanism.
- Separate the autonomous core (the functional need) from the mimetic margin (the status, the model) in any product's demand.
- Detect the convergence-rivalry transition: when the shared object starts being fought over, the market is entering the rivalry phase.
- Read the model chain: the trend's life is the life of its models' visibility, not the product's features.

## Phase 5 — Application and Design

- Position the brand in the model chain deliberately: be the model, be the model's choice, or be the aspirational object — and know which.
- For status goods, manage the distance: the model must remain slightly out of reach; total accessibility kills the mimetic margin.
- Use convergence deliberately: a visible model adopting the product is the strongest demand signal in the system.
- For rivalry-phase markets, design the distinction (the variant, the access tier) before the rivals do.

## Phase 6 — Ethics and Boundary Conditions

- Mimetic manipulation (manufacturing models to manufacture desire) is the demand analog of astroturfing; the line is whether the model's use is real and disclosed.
- Rivalry dynamics can escalate (copycat wars, price raids, scapegoating of a competitor); strategy that feeds the rivalry phase inherits its escalation risk.
- Mimetic demand is fickle by mechanism: the model moves, the object dies; the brand's identity must not be fused to one model.

## Phase 7 — Exercises and Assessment

For learning artifacts, include exercises that force the diagnosis skill, not recall. Use the standard set:

1. Map a real demand case onto the triangle — subject, model, object — and identify the model chain with evidence.
2. Decompose a product's demand into autonomous and mimetic components, with the measurement plan for each.
3. Track a trend's convergence: measure the object overlap across imitating groups over time and plot the convergence curve.
4. Detect the rivalry phase in a market: the price, exclusivity, and copycat signals — and the phase's strategic implications.
5. Design the model-chain position for a brand: the choice (model / model's choice / aspirational object), the distance management, and the risk note.
6. Write the ethics review for a model-driven demand campaign: the authenticity of the model's use, the disclosure, and the fickle-demand risk management.

For each exercise provide scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

## Phase 8 — Quality Gate and Delivery

Before delivering, run the delivery checklists. The artifact fails the gate on any “no.”

### Triangle mapping
- Subject, model, and object are named
- The model chain is traced
- The mimetic/autonomous split is quantified

### Phase diagnosis
- The convergence level is measured
- The rivalry signals are scored
- The model visibility trend is tracked

### Position design
- The model-chain role is chosen deliberately
- The distance is managed, not erased
- The fickle-demand exit is defined

### Delivery gate
- The model's use is real and disclosed
- The escalation risk is documented
- The next model-chain shift is monitored

## Anti-Patterns

demand attributed to product attributes without the triangle; the model chain assumed, not traced; distance erased (total accessibility); rivalry fed as strategy; the model's use manufactured; the brand fused to one model.

## Decision Heuristic

Before any claim or recommendation, ask:

1. Who is the model here, and is their desire for this real?
2. How much of this demand is mimetic, and how much autonomous?
3. Is the market converging, and are the rivalry signals appearing?
4. Where is the brand in the model chain, and is the distance managed?
5. If the model moved tomorrow, what of the demand would remain?

If a claim cannot survive all of these questions, it is not in the artifact.

## Final Principle

The goal is not to understand why people want things. It is to see the machine that makes them want — model, object, distance — and to position within it with clear eyes and clean hands.
