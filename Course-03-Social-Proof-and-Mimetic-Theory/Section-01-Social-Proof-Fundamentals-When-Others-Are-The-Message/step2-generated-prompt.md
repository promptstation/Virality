# Step 2 — Generated Prompt (Result of the Reusable Prompt)

```
Develop a comprehensive, professional-level learning module on **“Social Proof Fundamentals: When Others Are the Message”** as part of an advanced course on how others' behavior — observed, measured, and imitated — drives adoption, sharing, and trend formation. The module should how observed behavior functions as evidence — descriptive and injunctive norms — and the conditions under which proof moves behavior.

The material should teach the social-proof mechanism: behavior as evidence, the two norm types, and the credibility conditions (numbers, similarity, salience). Go beyond the assumption that arguments and information alone move behavior and examine the field experiments showing that norm messages — for and against — change behavior through proof, not persuasion.

Explain the core mechanism: under uncertainty, people look to the behavior of others as information; the observed behavior is the message, and its credibility comes from numbers, similarity, and salience.

Teach the two norm types — descriptive (what most people do) and injunctive (what most people approve of) — with the field evidence showing each works and that a descriptive norm stated against a high baseline can backfire.

Show the conditions: uncertainty is the engine (proof works most where the correct action is unclear), salience decides whether the proof is available, and similarity decides whether it applies.

Teach the failure modes: too few examples (proof needs a crowd or a credible few), the wrong reference group (proof from outsiders is discounted or resented), and salient counter-proof (a visible deviant outweights the described majority).

Introduce the measurement layer: adoption rates, conformity rates, and the experimental designs that separate proof from argument.

Include practical methods for working at a professional level:
* Auditing a communication for the norms it displays (descriptive, injunctive, counter-proof)
* Baseline measurement of the behavior (what is actually normal before the message)
* Field experiments: norm message versus control, with the backfire check
* Reference-group selection: who is the 'most people' that this audience believes?
* Salience design: where and how often the proof appears in the decision environment

Include practical exercises that require the learner to:
1. Audit a piece of marketing for the norms it displays — descriptive, injunctive, and any counter-proof — and score each on credibility.
2. Measure the baseline of a target behavior and write the norm message that is true, choosing descriptive versus injunctive with justification.
3. Design a field experiment: norm message versus control, the backfire check, the reference-group manipulation, and the analysis plan.
4. Diagnose a case where norm messaging failed: baseline, reference, salience, or counter-proof — and prescribe the fix.
5. Map the decision environment of a target behavior: where proof is currently visible, where it is absent, and the three highest-leverage placements.
6. Write the ethics review for a proof campaign: the authenticity of each proof element, the shaming check, and the line that would make it manipulation.

For each exercise, provide the scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

Structure the material progressively, beginning with the mechanism and the two norm types, moving through credibility conditions, failure modes, and measurement, then to a norm-message strategy built on a measured baseline. Establish clear conceptual distinctions between **descriptive norm, injunctive norm, reference group, and counter-proof** before showing how they interact.

Use professional terminology from social psychology, behavioral economics, and cultural theory where relevant, but explain specialized terminology in clear language. Do not make the material sound like generic AI-generated advice; the register should be that of an advanced professional training module used by behavioral researchers, brand strategists, and social scientists.

Research the subject using high-quality professional and academic sources where external research materially strengthens the material. Prioritize authoritative sources: the conformity literature (Asch, Sherif), Cialdini's social-proof research, the norm-influence literature, and Girard's mimetic theory with its anthropological and economic applications. Avoid relying primarily on low-quality SEO articles, generic social proof mimetic theory blogs, or unsupported “best practice” claims. Where useful, distinguish established research findings from professional conventions and informed recommendations.

Pay particular attention to the relationship between this module and principles such as **uncertainty as engine, similarity and numbers, salience at the decision point, and backfire from high baselines**. Connect these concepts to concrete analytical and design decisions rather than discussing them only theoretically.

Create a professional framework that behavioral researchers, brand strategists, and social scientists could actually use in a real project. The final material should therefore function simultaneously as a learning resource, a practical reference, and a working methodology for behavioral strategy, brand communication, and trend analysis.

Throughout the module, prioritize language that is:

* Precise without being jargon-heavy
* Empirical without being statistical theater
* Confident about what the evidence establishes
* Explicit about what the evidence does not establish
* Conditional where the findings are conditional
* Practical where the decisions are practical

Where a claim depends on context (platform, audience, topic, measurement), explicitly explain the trade-off and the boundary condition rather than presenting it as an absolute rule.

End the module with **Baseline audit checklist**, **Reference-group check checklist**, **Salience plan checklist**, and a **Delivery gate checklist** that a professional team can use before acting on behavioral strategy, brand communication, and trend analysis.

The final result should be comprehensive enough to serve as an advanced professional training module, but organized clearly enough that a learner can study it progressively and apply each concept in behavioral strategy, brand communication, and trend analysis.
```
