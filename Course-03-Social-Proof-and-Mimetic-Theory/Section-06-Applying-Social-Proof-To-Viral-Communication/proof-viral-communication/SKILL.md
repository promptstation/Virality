# Applying Social Proof to Viral Communication: Professional Module Builder

## Mission

Produce professional-level material on how to design social proof into viral communication — the platform instruments, the proof architecture, and the integration with referral and algorithmic systems. The unit of work is a defensible artifact — a learning module, an evidence brief, a case diagnosis, or a strategy memo — in which every claim is tied to a source, a mechanism, and a boundary condition.

Optimize simultaneously for:

- empirical precision (what the canonical studies and works actually found)
- conceptual clarity (the core terms kept strictly distinct)
- causal discipline (correlational and causal claims never blurred)
- practical utility (a practitioner can act on the output the same day)
- intellectual honesty (limits and boundary conditions as visible as the findings)

Do not produce generic advice or pop-science hype. The register is that of an advanced professional training program: confident where the evidence is strong, explicitly conditional where it is not.

## Use Cases

### New professional learning module
When asked to create a full module, course unit, or workshop on this topic:
1. Establish scope: audience, depth, and which claims the module must cover.
2. Build the evidence base from primary sources and canonical works.
3. Develop the core mechanisms and diagnostic logic.
4. Add method, critique, and application sections.
5. End with exercises and the delivery checklists.
6. Deliver the complete artifact with no placeholders.

### Brief or leadership memo
When asked for a short document that must decide something:
1. State the question the document must answer in one sentence.
2. Include only findings strong enough to bear that decision, each with its mechanism and source.
3. Convert results into plain-language implications.
4. End with the specific decisions the evidence changes.
5. Cap length; cut context before cutting caveats.

### Case diagnosis
When asked to explain why a specific piece of content, campaign, or product spread (or did not):
1. Identify the operative mechanisms — never a single assumed cause.
2. Map the case onto the diagnostic framework of this skill.
3. Run the differential: which mechanisms were active, which alternatives were considered and set aside.
4. State confidence and the evidence that would revise the diagnosis.

### Strategy design
When asked to design a campaign, content system, or program around this lever:
1. Fix the objective, audience, and platform context.
2. Select the mechanisms from this skill that fit that context.
3. Specify implementation, sequencing, and the measurement plan.
4. State the boundary conditions under which the plan breaks.

## Core Output Requirements

Regardless of format:

- Every named finding or claim carries its source or mechanism in the same breath.
- Correlational and causal claims are labeled as such wherever they matter.
- Effect claims carry their metric and a plain-language rendering.
- Boundary conditions are stated as explicit “applies when / breaks down when” conditions.
- Examples use real, named studies, campaigns, or artifacts — not invented case studies.
- No padding sections, generic intro/outro filler, or advice that would read the same for any topic.

## Phase 1 — Scope and Claim Audit

### Artifact and audience
What the user actually needs (module vs. memo vs. diagnosis) and who reads it. Different readers make different cuts of the same material.

### Claim inventory
List the claims the artifact will make. For each, note the source or mechanism that supports it and whether that support is correlational, experimental, or canonical. Downgrade or cut unsupported claims — not the caveat.

### Depth budget
Match length to the decision the artifact serves. The fix for a weak document is choosing stronger claims to carry it, not adding hedges to weaker ones.

## Phase 2 — Foundations and Core Content

1. Inventory the platform-native proof instruments: visible counts (likes, shares, followers), social actions (comments, tags, reactions), live signals (live viewers, trending), and reference framing ('people like you', 'your network') — each with its measured effect on uptake.
2. Teach the proof placement: the proof must be visible at the decision point (before the share, before the click, before the follow) — the placement rule and the platform's native slots for it.
3. Design the proof architecture of a viral asset: the evidence that rides with the content (the adoption curve visible in the asset, the named users, the running count) and the follow-up proof (the post-launch visible momentum).
4. Integrate with the referral loop (K-factor): the invited sees the proof of adoption at the landing — the proof that converts the cold visitor — and with the algorithm: the engagement counts are the proof the ranking system reads, and the proof and the algorithm are the same signal.
5. Build the proof calendar: the proof to build (generated, honest), the proof to borrow (partnership, press, community), and the proof never to fake (the non-negotiable line).

## Phase 3 — Method and Technique

- The platform-proof audit per asset: which instruments are present, their baseline, their placement
- Proof placement design: the decision point, the native slot, the format
- The referral-landing proof: the adoption evidence at the invited's landing, measured
- The algorithm-proof integration: the engagement signal the ranking reads, and the test of its effect
- The proof calendar: build / borrow / never-fake classification per campaign

## Phase 4 — Analysis and Diagnosis

- Diagnose the proof deficit per asset: instrument, placement, or timing — and prescribe the fix from the platform's native slots.
- Detect the proof that is invisible at the decision point (the count exists, the moment does not) and re-place it.
- Verify the referral integration: the invited's landing must show the adoption evidence, and the conversion must be measured with and without it.
- Read the algorithm loop: the engagement proof is driving the distribution, and the distribution is growing the proof — decompose the two.

## Phase 5 — Application and Design

- Place the strongest proof at the decision point, in the platform's native format, before the action.
- Design the asset to carry its evidence: the visible adoption that grows with it, not the claim that doesn't.
- Instrument the proof calendar before the campaign: what is built, what is borrowed, and the never-fake list.
- Close the loop with the referral landing: the proof that converts the invited, A/B tested.

## Phase 6 — Ethics and Boundary Conditions

- The never-fake line: purchased counts, bot engagement, and staged comments are the proof analog of fake reviews — the discovery cost is total.
- Proof that highlights a vulnerable group's non-adoption (shaming the laggard) is pressure, not evidence.
- The self-amplifying loop can be gamed (engagement bait); the line is whether the engagement is real and about the content.

## Phase 7 — Exercises and Assessment

For learning artifacts, include exercises that force the diagnosis skill, not recall. Use the standard set:

1. Audit five assets for proof architecture: instruments present, placement at the decision point, and the missing element — with the fix per asset.
2. Design the proof placement for a new asset: the decision point, the native slot, the format, and the baseline to beat.
3. Build the referral-landing proof: the adoption evidence, the A/B design, and the conversion endpoint.
4. Decompose an algorithm loop: the engagement-proof signal, the distribution effect, and the growth of the proof — with the numbers.
5. Draft the proof calendar for a quarter: the build / borrow / never-fake classification per item, with the source and the authenticity check.
6. Write the proof-ethics review: the never-fake list, the shaming check, and the engagement-bait line.

For each exercise provide scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

## Phase 8 — Quality Gate and Delivery

Before delivering, run the delivery checklists. The artifact fails the gate on any “no.”

### Placement audit
- The decision point is named per asset
- The native slot is used
- The baseline is measured

### Architecture
- The asset carries its own evidence
- The follow-up proof is scheduled
- The growth of the proof is visible

### Integration
- The referral landing shows the proof
- The A/B was run
- The algorithm loop is decomposed

### Delivery gate
- The never-fake list was checked
- The shaming check was run
- The next asset's proof is in the calendar

## Anti-Patterns

proof claimed but not placed; the count invisible at the decision point; the referral landing without proof; the loop read as organic; the purchased count; the laggard shamed.

## Decision Heuristic

Before any claim or recommendation, ask:

1. Is the proof visible at the exact moment of the decision?
2. Does the asset carry its own evidence, or only a claim?
3. Does the invited see the adoption, and was it measured?
4. Is the loop real, and is it decomposed?
5. Would every proof element survive a discovery audit?

If a claim cannot survive all of these questions, it is not in the artifact.

## Final Principle

The goal is not a bigger count. It is a decision point where the evidence is present, real, and visible — so that the crowd does the arguing, honestly.
