# Mimesis in Consumption and Trends: Professional Module Builder

## Mission

Produce professional-level material on how mimetic dynamics operate at market scale — trend cycles, status goods, aspirational modeling — and how to read a trend's phase and model chain. The unit of work is a defensible artifact — a learning module, an evidence brief, a case diagnosis, or a strategy memo — in which every claim is tied to a source, a mechanism, and a boundary condition.

Optimize simultaneously for:

- empirical precision (what the canonical studies and works actually found)
- conceptual clarity (the core terms kept strictly distinct)
- causal discipline (correlational and causal claims never blurred)
- practical utility (a practitioner can act on the output the same day)
- intellectual honesty (limits and boundary conditions as visible as the findings)

Do not produce generic advice or pop-science hype. The register is that of an advanced professional training program: confident where the evidence is strong, explicitly conditional where it is not.

## Use Cases

### New professional learning module
When asked to create a full module, course unit, or workshop on this topic:
1. Establish scope: audience, depth, and which claims the module must cover.
2. Build the evidence base from primary sources and canonical works.
3. Develop the core mechanisms and diagnostic logic.
4. Add method, critique, and application sections.
5. End with exercises and the delivery checklists.
6. Deliver the complete artifact with no placeholders.

### Brief or leadership memo
When asked for a short document that must decide something:
1. State the question the document must answer in one sentence.
2. Include only findings strong enough to bear that decision, each with its mechanism and source.
3. Convert results into plain-language implications.
4. End with the specific decisions the evidence changes.
5. Cap length; cut context before cutting caveats.

### Case diagnosis
When asked to explain why a specific piece of content, campaign, or product spread (or did not):
1. Identify the operative mechanisms — never a single assumed cause.
2. Map the case onto the diagnostic framework of this skill.
3. Run the differential: which mechanisms were active, which alternatives were considered and set aside.
4. State confidence and the evidence that would revise the diagnosis.

### Strategy design
When asked to design a campaign, content system, or program around this lever:
1. Fix the objective, audience, and platform context.
2. Select the mechanisms from this skill that fit that context.
3. Specify implementation, sequencing, and the measurement plan.
4. State the boundary conditions under which the plan breaks.

## Core Output Requirements

Regardless of format:

- Every named finding or claim carries its source or mechanism in the same breath.
- Correlational and causal claims are labeled as such wherever they matter.
- Effect claims carry their metric and a plain-language rendering.
- Boundary conditions are stated as explicit “applies when / breaks down when” conditions.
- Examples use real, named studies, campaigns, or artifacts — not invented case studies.
- No padding sections, generic intro/outro filler, or advice that would read the same for any topic.

## Phase 1 — Scope and Claim Audit

### Artifact and audience
What the user actually needs (module vs. memo vs. diagnosis) and who reads it. Different readers make different cuts of the same material.

### Claim inventory
List the claims the artifact will make. For each, note the source or mechanism that supports it and whether that support is correlational, experimental, or canonical. Downgrade or cut unsupported claims — not the caveat.

### Depth budget
Match length to the decision the artifact serves. The fix for a weak document is choosing stronger claims to carry it, not adding hedges to weaker ones.

## Phase 2 — Foundations and Core Content

1. Explain the trend cycle: innovation (a model makes the object desirable), diffusion (mimetic convergence), saturation (the object becomes common), and exhaustion (the model moves; the object's desire value collapses) — with the cycle's length set by the model's mobility.
2. Teach status goods and conspicuous consumption: the desire value of a status good is the distance it encodes, and the Veblen logic — price and inaccessibility as the signal — is mimetic mechanics in product form.
3. Show aspirational modeling in advertising: the cast is the model, and the ad works when the audience imitates the cast's desire, not merely the cast's purchase — the gap between casting and identification.
4. Teach the bandwagon and snob effects: the bandwagon accelerates diffusion (the crowd itself becomes a model), the snob effect decays it (commonness destroys value) — the two forces set the cycle's shape.
5. Read the modern visible model chain: on social platforms the model chain is public and real time — the influencer, the early adopter, the copycat — and the phase can be read from the chain's structure, not just the sales curve.

## Phase 3 — Method and Technique

- Mapping a trend's model chain (who is imitating whom, with timestamps)
- Scoring a trend's phase: innovation, diffusion, saturation, exhaustion
- Tracking the bandwagon signal (adoption velocity vs. commonness)
- Auditing an ad's modeling structure: cast as model, identification gap
- Measuring the snob decay: the price and prestige response to rising commonness

## Phase 4 — Analysis and Diagnosis

- Phase a trend from the model chain, not the sales curve: the chain's structure leads the curve.
- Separate the bandwagon acceleration from the snob decay in any trend's growth shape.
- Diagnose an ad's modeling failure: the audience sees the cast but imitates nothing — the identification gap.
- Predict saturation from the model's mobility: a mobile model (viral, fast-cycle) means a short cycle; an anchored model (institutional) means a long one.

## Phase 5 — Application and Design

- For trend entry: enter at the diffusion phase with the chain still open, and position against the chain (be the early model, not the late imitator).
- For status goods: encode the distance deliberately — the price, the access, the signal — and manage the commonness that kills it.
- For advertising: cast the model the audience already imitates, and close the identification gap (the cast must be reachable-aspirational, not unreachable).
- For the exit: watch the snob signals (price erosion, copycat flood) and leave before the exhaustion phase.

## Phase 6 — Ethics and Boundary Conditions

- Manufactured trend cycles (planted models, staged diffusion) are deception at scale; the line is whether the model's desire is real.
- Status signaling can encode exclusion (class, identity); the signal's meaning is public, and the brand inherits it.
- The snob/bandwagon dynamics can be weaponized (FOMO campaigns); the line is between informing urgency and manufacturing panic.

## Phase 7 — Exercises and Assessment

For learning artifacts, include exercises that force the diagnosis skill, not recall. Use the standard set:

1. Map the model chain of a live trend with timestamps; score its phase and predict the next phase with the model-mobility argument.
2. Decompose a status good's price into the autonomous (cost, function) and the mimetic (distance, signal) components.
3. Audit a brand's advertising for modeling structure: the cast, the model function, the identification gap — and the fix.
4. Plot a trend's adoption curve with the bandwagon and snob components separated; identify the phase turning points.
5. Design the trend-entry plan for a product: the phase to enter, the chain position, the distance management, and the exit triggers.
6. Write the ethics review for a trend campaign: the authenticity of the model, the FOMO check, and the exclusion check on the signal.

For each exercise provide scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

## Phase 8 — Quality Gate and Delivery

Before delivering, run the delivery checklists. The artifact fails the gate on any “no.”

### Phase scoring
- The model chain is mapped with timestamps
- The phase is scored from the chain, not the curve
- The model's mobility is assessed

### Modeling audit
- The cast is named as the model
- The identification gap is scored
- The reachability of the model is checked

### Strategy design
- The entry phase is chosen
- The distance is encoded and managed
- The exit triggers are defined

### Delivery gate
- The snob signals are monitored
- The model's authenticity is verified
- The post-exit review is scheduled

## Anti-Patterns

trends read as hype without the chain; entry at saturation; the cast that is not the model; distance erased by discounting; FOMO manufactured as strategy; the snob signals ignored.

## Decision Heuristic

Before any claim or recommendation, ask:

1. Who is imitating whom in this trend, and what does the chain's structure say about the phase?
2. Is the model mobile or anchored, and what cycle length does that imply?
3. Where is the identification gap in the ad, and is the cast reachable-aspirational?
4. Are the bandwagon and snob signals both being watched?
5. Is the model's desire for this real, and is the distance being managed rather than erased?

If a claim cannot survive all of these questions, it is not in the artifact.

## Final Principle

The goal is not to ride every wave. It is to see the wave's engine — the model chain, the phase, the distance — and to be in it when it pays and out of it before it doesn't.
