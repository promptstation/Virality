# Step 2 — Generated Prompt (Result of the Reusable Prompt)

```
Develop a comprehensive, professional-level learning module on **“From Pathogens to Ideas: Why Epidemiology Models Virality”** as part of an advanced course on how ideas and information spread through populations, using the mathematics of epidemics. The module should how the epidemic model maps onto the spread of ideas — the valid analogies, the breaks, and the right unit of analysis.

The material should teach the translation layer: epidemic concepts (exposure, infection, recovery) mapped onto information diffusion, with the analogy's limits stated. Go beyond the slogan 'viral spread is just like a disease' without knowing what the model actually says and examine where the analogy holds (threshold dynamics, saturation) and where it breaks (re-exposure, algorithms, fatigue).

Explain the historical origin: Kermack and McKendrick's 1927 model of infectious disease — the first compartment model — and the line of development from it to modern diffusion modeling.

Define the units: an individual (state carrier), a content (the 'pathogen'), and the network (the contact structure) — and when each is the right unit of analysis.

Map the states: Susceptible (can be exposed), Infectious (actively spreading), Recovered (no longer spreading) onto awareness, active sharing, and fatigue/uninterest.

State the valid analogies: the threshold dynamic (an idea below a critical rate dies out), the saturation curve, and the effect of removing spreaders.

State the breaks: ideas are re-encountered (SIS-like), 'immunity' is topic fatigue not biology, and platform ranking acts as an external forcing on contact rates.

Include practical methods for working at a professional level:
* Choosing the unit of analysis from the research question
* Defining the states for a specific content case (what 'infected' and 'recovered' mean here)
* A validity checklist for the pathogen analogy on a given dataset
* Historical grounding: the Kermack–McKendrick derivation in one page
* The break-list review: which deviations from the classic model this case requires

Include practical exercises that require the learner to:
1. Take a real diffusion case (a campaign, a product launch) and produce the mapping: unit, states, contact structure, and the break-list.
2. Write the one-page Kermack–McKendrick derivation and annotate each equation with its information-diffusion reading.
3. Classify five diffusion cases by state structure: SIR (one-shot), SIS (re-exposure), or something outside the family.
4. Produce the validity checklist verdict for a case where the platform algorithm is the dominant contact driver.
5. Design the 'recovered' definition for a news brand (what ends a reader's spread of a story) and defend it.
6. Write the modeling brief for a product-launch diffusion: unit, states, contact structure, break-list, base model choice, and why.

For each exercise, provide the scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

Structure the material progressively, beginning with the history and the conceptual mapping, moving through units, states, and the analogy's limits, then to a modeling brief ready for parameter estimation. Establish clear conceptual distinctions between **individual, content, network, exposure, adoption, and fatigue** before showing how they interact.

Use professional terminology from epidemiology, mathematical modeling, and diffusion science where relevant, but explain specialized terminology in clear language. Do not make the material sound like generic AI-generated advice; the register should be that of an advanced professional training module used by quantitative marketers, growth analysts, and communication researchers.

Research the subject using high-quality professional and academic sources where external research materially strengthens the material. Prioritize authoritative sources: the classic epidemic-modeling literature (Kermack and McKendrick 1927 onward), the SIR/SEIR compartment-model literature, network-based diffusion models, and the computational epidemiology textbooks and toolkits. Avoid relying primarily on low-quality SEO articles, generic sir epidemiology blogs, or unsupported “best practice” claims. Where useful, distinguish established research findings from professional conventions and informed recommendations.

Pay particular attention to the relationship between this module and principles such as **unit of analysis, state definition, threshold intuition, and analogy breaks**. Connect these concepts to concrete analytical and design decisions rather than discussing them only theoretically.

Create a professional framework that quantitative marketers, growth analysts, and communication researchers could actually use in a real project. The final material should therefore function simultaneously as a learning resource, a practical reference, and a working methodology for predicting and steering the spread of content, products, and ideas.

Throughout the module, prioritize language that is:

* Precise without being jargon-heavy
* Empirical without being statistical theater
* Confident about what the evidence establishes
* Explicit about what the evidence does not establish
* Conditional where the findings are conditional
* Practical where the decisions are practical

Where a claim depends on context (platform, audience, topic, measurement), explicitly explain the trade-off and the boundary condition rather than presenting it as an absolute rule.

End the module with **Mapping audit checklist**, **Analogy validity checklist**, **Historical grounding checklist**, and a **Delivery gate checklist** that a professional team can use before acting on predicting and steering the spread of content, products, and ideas.

The final result should be comprehensive enough to serve as an advanced professional training module, but organized clearly enough that a learner can study it progressively and apply each concept in predicting and steering the spread of content, products, and ideas.
```
