# From Pathogens to Ideas: Why Epidemiology Models Virality: Professional Module Builder

## Mission

Produce professional-level material on how the epidemic model maps onto the spread of ideas — the valid analogies, the breaks, and the right unit of analysis. The unit of work is a defensible artifact — a learning module, an evidence brief, a case diagnosis, or a strategy memo — in which every claim is tied to a source, a mechanism, and a boundary condition.

Optimize simultaneously for:

- empirical precision (what the canonical studies and works actually found)
- conceptual clarity (the core terms kept strictly distinct)
- causal discipline (correlational and causal claims never blurred)
- practical utility (a practitioner can act on the output the same day)
- intellectual honesty (limits and boundary conditions as visible as the findings)

Do not produce generic advice or pop-science hype. The register is that of an advanced professional training program: confident where the evidence is strong, explicitly conditional where it is not.

## Use Cases

### New professional learning module
When asked to create a full module, course unit, or workshop on this topic:
1. Establish scope: audience, depth, and which claims the module must cover.
2. Build the evidence base from primary sources and canonical works.
3. Develop the core mechanisms and diagnostic logic.
4. Add method, critique, and application sections.
5. End with exercises and the delivery checklists.
6. Deliver the complete artifact with no placeholders.

### Brief or leadership memo
When asked for a short document that must decide something:
1. State the question the document must answer in one sentence.
2. Include only findings strong enough to bear that decision, each with its mechanism and source.
3. Convert results into plain-language implications.
4. End with the specific decisions the evidence changes.
5. Cap length; cut context before cutting caveats.

### Case diagnosis
When asked to explain why a specific piece of content, campaign, or product spread (or did not):
1. Identify the operative mechanisms — never a single assumed cause.
2. Map the case onto the diagnostic framework of this skill.
3. Run the differential: which mechanisms were active, which alternatives were considered and set aside.
4. State confidence and the evidence that would revise the diagnosis.

### Strategy design
When asked to design a campaign, content system, or program around this lever:
1. Fix the objective, audience, and platform context.
2. Select the mechanisms from this skill that fit that context.
3. Specify implementation, sequencing, and the measurement plan.
4. State the boundary conditions under which the plan breaks.

## Core Output Requirements

Regardless of format:

- Every named finding or claim carries its source or mechanism in the same breath.
- Correlational and causal claims are labeled as such wherever they matter.
- Effect claims carry their metric and a plain-language rendering.
- Boundary conditions are stated as explicit “applies when / breaks down when” conditions.
- Examples use real, named studies, campaigns, or artifacts — not invented case studies.
- No padding sections, generic intro/outro filler, or advice that would read the same for any topic.

## Phase 1 — Scope and Claim Audit

### Artifact and audience
What the user actually needs (module vs. memo vs. diagnosis) and who reads it. Different readers make different cuts of the same material.

### Claim inventory
List the claims the artifact will make. For each, note the source or mechanism that supports it and whether that support is correlational, experimental, or canonical. Downgrade or cut unsupported claims — not the caveat.

### Depth budget
Match length to the decision the artifact serves. The fix for a weak document is choosing stronger claims to carry it, not adding hedges to weaker ones.

## Phase 2 — Foundations and Core Content

1. Explain the historical origin: Kermack and McKendrick's 1927 model of infectious disease — the first compartment model — and the line of development from it to modern diffusion modeling.
2. Define the units: an individual (state carrier), a content (the 'pathogen'), and the network (the contact structure) — and when each is the right unit of analysis.
3. Map the states: Susceptible (can be exposed), Infectious (actively spreading), Recovered (no longer spreading) onto awareness, active sharing, and fatigue/uninterest.
4. State the valid analogies: the threshold dynamic (an idea below a critical rate dies out), the saturation curve, and the effect of removing spreaders.
5. State the breaks: ideas are re-encountered (SIS-like), 'immunity' is topic fatigue not biology, and platform ranking acts as an external forcing on contact rates.

## Phase 3 — Method and Technique

- Choosing the unit of analysis from the research question
- Defining the states for a specific content case (what 'infected' and 'recovered' mean here)
- A validity checklist for the pathogen analogy on a given dataset
- Historical grounding: the Kermack–McKendrick derivation in one page
- The break-list review: which deviations from the classic model this case requires

## Phase 4 — Analysis and Diagnosis

- Check the analogy before modeling: does this case have re-exposure, algorithmic forcing, or rapid fatigue? Each mandates a model variant.
- Diagnose a 'dead' diffusion (the idea stopped early) as a threshold failure, not a content failure, before prescribing changes.
- Classify the population: is 'recovery' one-shot (a story consumed) or recurrent (a category followed)?
- Flag when the epidemic frame is the wrong frame (one-time event, pure paid reach) and name the right one.

## Phase 5 — Application and Design

- Write the modeling brief: unit, states, contact structure, and the break-list for the analogy.
- Choose the base model (SIR, SIS, SEIR) from the break-list, not from habit.
- Define 'recovered' operationally with the data team before any parameter is estimated.
- Sequence: conceptual map first, then the model — a model built on a broken map forecasts the wrong future.

## Phase 6 — Ethics and Boundary Conditions

- The analogy can mislead strategically: 'we are at the peak' read from a disease curve can cut a diffusion that is structurally two-wave.
- Treating fatigue as immunity (one-shot recovery) overstates the final size when re-exposure is real.
- Modeling people as uniform ignores the heterogeneity that drives super-spreading; the ethical and strategic cost is mis-allocated spend.

## Phase 7 — Exercises and Assessment

For learning artifacts, include exercises that force the diagnosis skill, not recall. Use the standard set:

1. Take a real diffusion case (a campaign, a product launch) and produce the mapping: unit, states, contact structure, and the break-list.
2. Write the one-page Kermack–McKendrick derivation and annotate each equation with its information-diffusion reading.
3. Classify five diffusion cases by state structure: SIR (one-shot), SIS (re-exposure), or something outside the family.
4. Produce the validity checklist verdict for a case where the platform algorithm is the dominant contact driver.
5. Design the 'recovered' definition for a news brand (what ends a reader's spread of a story) and defend it.
6. Write the modeling brief for a product-launch diffusion: unit, states, contact structure, break-list, base model choice, and why.

For each exercise provide scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

## Phase 8 — Quality Gate and Delivery

Before delivering, run the delivery checklists. The artifact fails the gate on any “no.”

### Mapping audit
- The unit of analysis is chosen and justified
- The states are defined operationally
- The contact structure is named

### Analogy validity
- The break-list is complete
- The base model follows from the breaks
- The wrong-frame check was run

### Historical grounding
- The Kermack–McKendrick origin is stated
- The development line to the chosen model is traced
- Each deviation from the classic model is named

### Delivery gate
- The modeling brief is approved
- The 'recovered' definition is agreed with data
- The first estimation step is scheduled

## Anti-Patterns

the unexamined pathogen slogan; uniform populations as a default; fatigue treated as permanent immunity; the algorithm ignored as an external force; modeling before the unit is chosen; the threshold result quoted without its derivation.

## Decision Heuristic

Before any claim or recommendation, ask:

1. What is the unit, and why is it the right one for this question?
2. What exactly counts as 'recovered' here, and who signed off on that definition?
3. Which breaks from the classic model does this case require?
4. Is the threshold dynamic actually in play, or is this pure paid reach?
5. Would the forecast change if re-exposure were allowed?

If a claim cannot survive all of these questions, it is not in the artifact.

## Final Principle

The goal is not to make every campaign sound like an epidemic. It is to know, before the model is built, exactly where the epidemic logic applies — and to build only what that knowledge justifies.
