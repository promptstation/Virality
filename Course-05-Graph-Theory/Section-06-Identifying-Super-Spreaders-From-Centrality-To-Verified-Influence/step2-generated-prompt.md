# Step 2 — Generated Prompt (Result of the Reusable Prompt)

```
Develop a comprehensive, professional-level learning module on **“Identifying Super-Spreaders: From Centrality to Verified Influence”** as part of an advanced course on the structure of social networks and how that structure determines who spreads what, where, and how fast. The module should how to separate the structurally central from the behaviorally super — the verified super-spreader — and to allocate the seeding budget with a validation design.

The material should teach the verification layer: the structure-behavior-time-quality stack that produces the verified list, and the budget logic that spends on it. Go beyond the follower-count leaderboard as the seeding list and examine the heavy tail of real sharing behavior and the failure modes that a leaderboard cannot see.

State the evidence: the sharing behavior in real social data is a heavy tail — a small fraction of nodes accounts for a disproportionate share of the propagation — and the super-spreader question is the operational version of the tail.

Teach the verification stack, layer by layer: the structural position (the centrality profile), the observed propagation (the historical propagation from this node's content — the median and the tail of its own spread distribution), the temporal activity (the activity at the relevant time window), and the audience quality (the overlap with the target, the engagement depth, the cross-posting).

Cover the failure modes as the diagnosis of each layer's absence: the dormant hub (structure without activity), the echo-chamber amplifier (propagation without crossing), the bought audience (numbers without quality), and the one-hit wonder (a tail event without a distribution).

Teach the cost model: the reach per unit of spend (or effort) per node, the audience-overlap discount (the reach already reached is worth less), and the diminishing returns within a node (the second campaign to the same audience).

Build the seeding decision: the verified list ranked by the expected incremental reach per cost, the budget allocation (the concentrated vs. the diversified rule, from the risk of the single-point failure), and the validation design (the control group, the measurement window, the incremental-reach estimate).

Include practical methods for working at a professional level:
* The propagation-distribution build: per-node spread statistics from the historical data
* The four-layer verification profile per candidate
* The incremental-reach model: the overlap discount, the cost per incremental reach
* The allocation rule: the concentration-diversification split with the single-point risk bound
* The validation design: the control, the window, the incremental estimate, the decision rule

Include practical exercises that require the learner to:
1. Build the propagation-distribution table for a candidate pool: the median, the tail, the variance — and the super-spreader classification from the distribution.
2. Run the four-layer stack on ten candidates; produce the verified list and the diagnosis for each rejected one (the missing layer).
3. Compute the incremental-reach model for the verified list: the overlap discounts, the cost per incremental reach, and the ranking.
4. Allocate a fixed budget: the concentration-diversification split, the risk bound, and the alternative allocation under the adverse case (the top node declines).
5. Design the validation: the control group, the measurement window, the incremental estimate, and the decision rule for the next round.
6. Write the market-signal and consent memo for the seeding list: what is shared, the disclosure plan, the data-minimization statement.

For each exercise, provide the scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

Structure the material progressively, beginning with the heavy-tail evidence and the verification stack, moving through the failure modes and the cost model, then to the verified list, the allocation, and the validation. Establish clear conceptual distinctions between **position, propagation, timing, quality, and incremental reach** before showing how they interact.

Use professional terminology from graph theory, network science, and social network analysis where relevant, but explain specialized terminology in clear language. Do not make the material sound like generic AI-generated advice; the register should be that of an advanced professional training module used by growth analysts, social researchers, and data scientists working with network data.

Research the subject using high-quality professional and academic sources where external research materially strengthens the material. Prioritize authoritative sources: the network-science literature (Watts and Strogatz 1998, Barabási and Albert 1999, the Watts small-world and Barabási scale-free papers), the centrality and community-detection literature, the diffusion-on-networks models (independent cascade, linear threshold), and the standard network-analysis toolkits. Avoid relying primarily on low-quality SEO articles, generic graph theory networks blogs, or unsupported “best practice” claims. Where useful, distinguish established research findings from professional conventions and informed recommendations.

Pay particular attention to the relationship between this module and principles such as **the distribution over the event, the incremental standard, the risk bound, and the validation loop**. Connect these concepts to concrete analytical and design decisions rather than discussing them only theoretically.

Create a professional framework that growth analysts, social researchers, and data scientists working with network data could actually use in a real project. The final material should therefore function simultaneously as a learning resource, a practical reference, and a working methodology for targeting, seeding, and analyzing spread on social networks.

Throughout the module, prioritize language that is:

* Precise without being jargon-heavy
* Empirical without being statistical theater
* Confident about what the evidence establishes
* Explicit about what the evidence does not establish
* Conditional where the findings are conditional

Where a claim depends on context (platform, audience, topic, measurement), explicitly explain the trade-off and the boundary condition rather than presenting it as an absolute rule.

End the module with **Verification checklist**, **Cost model checklist**, **Allocation checklist**, and a **Validation checklist** that a professional team can use before acting on targeting, seeding, and analyzing spread on social networks.
```
