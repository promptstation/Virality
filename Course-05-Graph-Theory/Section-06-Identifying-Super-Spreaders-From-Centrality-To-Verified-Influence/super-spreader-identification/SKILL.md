# Identifying Super-Spreaders: From Centrality to Verified Influence: Professional Module Builder

## Mission

Produce professional-level material on how to separate the structurally central from the behaviorally super — the verified super-spreader — and to allocate the seeding budget with a validation design. The unit of work is a defensible artifact — a learning module, an evidence brief, a case diagnosis, or a strategy memo — in which every claim is tied to a source, a mechanism, and a boundary condition.

Optimize simultaneously for:

- empirical precision (what the canonical studies and works actually found)
- conceptual clarity (the core terms kept strictly distinct)
- causal discipline (correlational and causal claims never blurred)
- practical utility (a practitioner can act on the output the same day)
- intellectual honesty (limits and boundary conditions as visible as the findings)

Do not produce generic advice or pop-science hype. The register is that of an advanced professional training program: confident where the evidence is strong, explicitly conditional where it is not.

## Use Cases

### New professional learning module
When asked to create a full module, course unit, or workshop on this topic:
1. Establish scope: audience, depth, and which claims the module must cover.
2. Build the evidence base from primary sources and canonical works.
3. Develop the core mechanisms and diagnostic logic.
4. Add method, critique, and application sections.
5. End with exercises and the delivery checklists.
6. Deliver the complete artifact with no placeholders.

### Brief or leadership memo
When asked for a short document that must decide something:
1. State the question the document must answer in one sentence.
2. Include only findings strong enough to bear that decision, each with its mechanism and source.
3. Convert results into plain-language implications.
4. End with the specific decisions the evidence changes.
5. Cap length; cut context before cutting caveats.

### Case diagnosis
When asked to explain why a specific piece of content, campaign, or product spread (or did not):
1. Identify the operative mechanisms — never a single assumed cause.
2. Map the case onto the diagnostic framework of this skill.
3. Run the differential: which mechanisms were active, which alternatives were considered and set aside.
4. State confidence and the evidence that would revise the diagnosis.

### Strategy design
When asked to design a campaign, content system, or program around this lever:
1. Fix the objective, audience, and platform context.
2. Select the mechanisms from this skill that fit that context.
3. Specify implementation, sequencing, and the measurement plan.
4. State the boundary conditions under which the plan breaks.

## Core Output Requirements

Regardless of format:

- Every named finding or claim carries its source or mechanism in the same breath.
- Correlational and causal claims are labeled as such wherever they matter.
- Effect claims carry their metric and a plain-language rendering.
- Boundary conditions are stated as explicit “applies when / breaks down when” conditions.
- Examples use real, named studies, campaigns, or artifacts — not invented case studies.
- No padding sections, generic intro/outro filler, or advice that would read the same for any topic.

## Phase 1 — Scope and Claim Audit

### Artifact and audience
What the user actually needs (module vs. memo vs. diagnosis) and who reads it. Different readers make different cuts of the same material.

### Claim inventory
List the claims the artifact will make. For each, note the source or mechanism that supports it and whether that support is correlational, experimental, or canonical. Downgrade or cut unsupported claims — not the caveat.

### Depth budget
Match length to the decision the artifact serves. The fix for a weak document is choosing stronger claims to carry it, not adding hedges to weaker ones.

## Phase 2 — Foundations and Core Content

1. State the evidence: the sharing behavior in real social data is a heavy tail — a small fraction of nodes accounts for a disproportionate share of the propagation — and the super-spreader question is the operational version of the tail.
2. Teach the verification stack, layer by layer: the structural position (the centrality profile), the observed propagation (the historical propagation from this node's content — the median and the tail of its own spread distribution), the temporal activity (the activity at the relevant time window), and the audience quality (the overlap with the target, the engagement depth, the cross-posting).
3. Cover the failure modes as the diagnosis of each layer's absence: the dormant hub (structure without activity), the echo-chamber amplifier (propagation without crossing), the bought audience (numbers without quality), and the one-hit wonder (a tail event without a distribution).
4. Teach the cost model: the reach per unit of spend (or effort) per node, the audience-overlap discount (the reach already reached is worth less), and the diminishing returns within a node (the second campaign to the same audience).
5. Build the seeding decision: the verified list ranked by the expected incremental reach per cost, the budget allocation (the concentrated vs. the diversified rule, from the risk of the single-point failure), and the validation design (the control group, the measurement window, the incremental-reach estimate).

## Phase 3 — Method and Technique

- The propagation-distribution build: per-node spread statistics from the historical data
- The four-layer verification profile per candidate
- The incremental-reach model: the overlap discount, the cost per incremental reach
- The allocation rule: the concentration-diversification split with the single-point risk bound
- The validation design: the control, the window, the incremental estimate, the decision rule

## Phase 4 — Analysis and Diagnosis

- Diagnose the seeding miss by layer: which of the four was missing in the failed seed — the position, the propagation, the timing, or the quality?
- Read the distribution, not the event: a node with one viral post and a flat rest is a lottery ticket, not a super-spreader; the median and the variance decide.
- Check the incremental model: the seed's audience overlap with the already-reached set — the gross reach minus the overlap is the number that pays.
- Detect the timing failure: the structurally and behaviorally right node, active elsewhere at the launch window — the position held, the moment missed.

## Phase 5 — Application and Design

- Run the verification stack on the candidate pool before any spend; the unverified list is a hypothesis, the verified list is the investment.
- Allocate by the incremental cost: the ranking is the expected incremental reach per cost, and the top of the list is not automatically the whole budget.
- Apply the concentration rule with the risk bound: the top node carries at most the share that its single-point failure can absorb, and the rest is diversified across the verified tier.
- Validate as designed: the control, the window, the incremental estimate — the result updates the node's profile for the next round.

## Phase 6 — Ethics and Boundary Conditions

- The seeding list is a public signal (the listed nodes know they are on it); the list's handling — what is shared, what is not, and with whom — is a market decision.
- The paid amplification of a node's content changes the node's incentives and its audience's trust; the disclosure of the paid element is the line, and the audience's right to know is the standard.
- The verification data (the node's propagation history) is personal data about the node's audience; the processing is bound by the consent and the minimization rules.

## Phase 7 — Exercises and Assessment

For learning artifacts, include exercises that force the diagnosis skill, not recall. Use the standard set:

1. Build the propagation-distribution table for a candidate pool: the median, the tail, the variance — and the super-spreader classification from the distribution.
2. Run the four-layer stack on ten candidates; produce the verified list and the diagnosis for each rejected one (the missing layer).
3. Compute the incremental-reach model for the verified list: the overlap discounts, the cost per incremental reach, and the ranking.
4. Allocate a fixed budget: the concentration-diversification split, the risk bound, and the alternative allocation under the adverse case (the top node declines).
5. Design the validation: the control group, the measurement window, the incremental estimate, and the decision rule for the next round.
6. Write the market-signal and consent memo for the seeding list: what is shared, the disclosure plan, the data-minimization statement.

For each exercise provide scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

## Phase 8 — Quality Gate and Delivery

Before delivering, run the delivery checklists. The artifact fails the gate on any “no.”

### Verification
- The four layers were computed per candidate
- The distribution (not the event) was read
- The rejections carry their diagnosis

### Cost model
- The overlap discount is applied
- The cost per incremental reach is the ranking
- The diminishing returns are modeled

### Allocation
- The risk bound is stated
- The adverse-case alternative is named
- The diversification is explicit

### Validation
- The control and the window are set
- The incremental estimate is the decision metric
- The profile update is scheduled

## Anti-Patterns

the leaderboard as the list; the one-hit wonder bought as a super-spreader; the gross reach reported instead of the incremental; the whole budget on one node; the timing ignored; the disclosure skipped.

## Decision Heuristic

Before any claim or recommendation, ask:

1. What does this node's distribution say — the median, the tail, the variance?
2. Which layer is missing, if the node underperforms?
3. What is the incremental reach after the overlap, and what does it cost?
4. Can the plan absorb the top node's failure?
5. Will the validation tell us what happened, by design?

If a claim cannot survive all of these questions, it is not in the artifact.

## Final Principle

The goal is not a longer list. It is a verified list, an incremental budget, a risk bound, and a validation that learns — so the spend goes to the nodes that are placed, proven, timely, and worth the cost.
