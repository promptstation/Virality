Develop a comprehensive, professional-level learning module on **“Optimizing i: Invite Volume, Shareable Surfaces, and the Spam Line”** as part of an advanced course on the mathematics of product-led viral growth — the referral loop, its coefficient, and the economics of compounding. The module should how i is raised — the surfaces, the features, the frequency architecture — and the spam line that bounds the volume.

Go beyond the 'add more share buttons' version of volume optimization and examine the marginal invite's decay and the sender tail — the volume that converts and the volume that costs.

Teach the shareable surfaces: the product's outputs (the created artifact shared as the proof), the achievements (the result shared as the win), the collaborations (the shared workspace that requires the other) — the surfaces where the send is the product's behavior, not a campaign's ask, and the design of each (the default payload, the friction of the send, the channel).

Cover the structural features: the multi-user features (the product that needs others to be fully useful — the document, the team, the game), the gifting (the value that travels as the invitation), the challenges (the competitive loop that the scoreboard completes) — the features that raise i by changing the product's use, not by asking for the send.

Teach the frequency architecture: the send budget per user (the cap, the cooldown), the per-surface frequency (the collaboration invite is a different animal from the achievement share), and the state logic (the send offered when the value is fresh, not on a schedule) — the architecture that keeps the volume inside the goodwill.

Cover the volume-quality trade-off with the evidence: the marginal invite's conversion decays with the sender's cumulative sends (the first invite converts, the fifth is noise), the sender tail (the small fraction of high-volume senders who drive most of i and a disproportionate share of the complaints), and the read: the i optimization has a diminishing-return curve, and the knee is where the strategy stops pushing volume.

Teach the spam line's monitors: the opt-out rate (the invitee's rejection of the sender's world), the unfriend/unfollow rate (the relationship damage), the report rate, and the platform's rate limits (the external bound) — the four signals that define the line, and the action rule when one crosses.

Build the i-health measurement: the distribution of sends per user (the median, the tail), the send retention (the senders who keep sending, and the cohort that churns from sending), and the per-surface i decomposition — the standing series that prices the volume.

* The surface audit: the product's surfaces, the send friction per surface, the default payload per surface

* The marginal-decay curve: the conversion per send-number, per referrer cohort
* The i-health dashboard: the distribution, the retention, the per-surface series, the four spam-line monitors

3. Design the frequency architecture: the caps, the cooldowns, the state logic, per surface — with the opt-out and the standing preference.

5. Audit the sender tail: the top senders' share, their invitees' outcomes, the opt-out exposure — and the intervention spec.
6. Write the spam-line policy: the four monitors, the thresholds, the action rules, and the user-facing controls — as the standing document.

For each exercise, provide the scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

Structure the material progressively, beginning with the surfaces and the structural features, moving through the frequency, the decay, and the tail, then to the i strategy inside the spam line, instrumented. Establish clear conceptual distinctions between **surface, structural feature, frequency, marginal invite, and sender tail** before showing how they interact.
