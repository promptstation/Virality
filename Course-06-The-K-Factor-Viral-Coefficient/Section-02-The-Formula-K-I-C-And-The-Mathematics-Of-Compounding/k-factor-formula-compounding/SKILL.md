# The Formula: k = i × c, and the Mathematics of Compounding: Professional Module Builder

## Mission

Produce professional-level material on the K-factor's arithmetic — the formula, the compounding it implies, the k>1 and k<1 trajectories, and the sensitivity of k to its two levers. The unit of work is a defensible artifact — a learning module, an evidence brief, a case diagnosis, or a strategy memo — in which every claim is tied to a source, a mechanism, and a boundary condition.

Optimize simultaneously for:

- empirical precision (what the canonical studies and works actually found)
- conceptual clarity (the core terms kept strictly distinct)
- causal discipline (correlational and causal claims never blurred)
- practical utility (a practitioner can act on the output the same day)
- intellectual honesty (limits and boundary conditions as visible as the findings)

Do not produce generic advice or pop-science hype. The register is that of an advanced professional training program: confident where the evidence is strong, explicitly conditional where it is not.

## Use Cases

### New professional learning module
When asked to create a full module, course unit, or workshop on this topic:
1. Establish scope: audience, depth, and which claims the module must cover.
2. Build the evidence base from primary sources and canonical works.
3. Develop the core mechanisms and diagnostic logic.
4. Add method, critique, and application sections.
5. End with exercises and the delivery checklists.
6. Deliver the complete artifact with no placeholders.

### Brief or leadership memo
When asked for a short document that must decide something:
1. State the question the document must answer in one sentence.
2. Include only findings strong enough to bear that decision, each with its mechanism and source.
3. Convert results into plain-language implications.
4. End with the specific decisions the evidence changes.
5. Cap length; cut context before cutting caveats.

### Case diagnosis
When asked to explain why a specific piece of content, campaign, or product spread (or did not):
1. Identify the operative mechanisms — never a single assumed cause.
2. Map the case onto the diagnostic framework of this skill.
3. Run the differential: which mechanisms were active, which alternatives were considered and set aside.
4. State confidence and the evidence that would revise the diagnosis.

### Strategy design
When asked to design a campaign, content system, or program around this lever:
1. Fix the objective, audience, and platform context.
2. Select the mechanisms from this skill that fit that context.
3. Specify implementation, sequencing, and the measurement plan.
4. State the boundary conditions under which the plan breaks.

## Phase 1 — Scope and Claim Audit

### Artifact and audience
What the user actually needs (module vs. memo vs. diagnosis) and who reads it. Different readers make different cuts of the same material.

### Claim inventory
List the claims the artifact will make. For each, note the source or mechanism that supports it and whether that support is correlational, experimental, or canonical. Downgrade or cut unsupported claims — not the caveat.

### Depth budget
Match length to the decision the artifact serves. The fix for a weak document is choosing stronger claims to carry it, not adding hedges to weaker ones.

## Phase 2 — Foundations and Core Content

1. Define the factors: i (invites per user — the volume of the loop's output) and c (the invite-to-user conversion — the quality of the loop's input), and k = i × c (the net new users per existing user per cycle).
2. Derive the recursion: the users at the next cycle from the current users (the loop's addition) plus the exogenous input (the paid, the organic) — the full growth equation, of which k is one term.
3. Work the k > 1 case: the exponential regime, the doubling time from k, and the practical limits (the market size, the saturation, the quality of the referred) that the pure exponential ignores.
4. Work the k < 1 case: the convergence — the loop's contribution as a geometric series with a finite ceiling (the total loop-driven users as a multiple of the seed), and the reading that the loop is a multiplier on the paid input, with the multiple = 1/(1−k).
5. Teach the sensitivity: the partial effect of i and of c on k, the asymmetry (a 10% move in i versus in c), and the cost structure that decides which lever to pull (the invite volume is cheap and spammy; the conversion is expensive and product-quality).

## Phase 3 — Method and Technique

- The K-factor computation from the invite and the conversion data (per cycle, per cohort)
- The trajectory simulation: the recursion with the exogenous input, the k>1 and k<1 cases plotted
- The doubling-time and the time-to-scale computations from k
- The ceiling computation for the k<1 case (the multiple on the seed, the multiple on the paid)
- The sensitivity table: k as a function of i and c, with the cost notes per lever

## Phase 4 — Analysis and Diagnosis

- Read the trajectory, not the point: the same k at different time horizons is a different strategy (the k=1.2 case is a different animal at month 3 than at month 12, where the saturation hits).
- Diagnose the lever from the decomposition: a low k from a low i (the moment is weak, the share is rare) versus a low k from a low c (the invitation is weak, the landing is cold) — the two prescriptions are opposite.
- Check the quality of the c: a conversion that is high but whose referred do not retain is a loop that buys churn — the retention-qualified c is the honest factor.
- Separate the cycle definitions: the k computed on weekly cycles versus monthly cycles differs, and the trajectory claim must state the cycle.

## Phase 5 — Application and Design

- Compute k per cohort and per cycle, not once: the K-factor is a time series, and its drift (the decay of the share habit, the learning of the landing) is the standing signal.
- Set the trajectory target from the business case: the time-to-scale the funding or the market window allows, back-solved to the required k — the target k is the design input.
- Choose the lever from the sensitivity and the cost: the i lever for the moment problem, the c lever for the invitation problem — and the sequence (the c first, where the landing is the bottleneck).
- Model the ceiling explicitly for the sub-1 case: the loop's multiple on the paid input is the number the budget decision uses.

## Phase 6 — Ethics and Boundary Conditions

- The i lever (more invites) has a user-experience cost (the spam perception, the unfriend); the line is the opt-out rate and the relationship damage, measured, not assumed.
- The c lever can be gamed (the dark-pattern landing that converts and then disappoints); the retention of the referred is the check that the conversion was honest.
- The trajectory model's parameters (the exogenous input, the saturation) encode assumptions about the market; the claim inherits the assumptions, and the sensitivity to them is part of the forecast.

## Phase 7 — Exercises and Assessment

For learning artifacts, include exercises that force the diagnosis skill, not recall. Use the standard set:

1. Compute k for a product from the invite and conversion data, per cohort over six cycles; plot the drift and diagnose the trend (the moment decay, the landing improvement).
2. Simulate the trajectory for k = 0.6, 0.9, 1.0, 1.2, 1.5 with the same exogenous input; mark the doubling times and the saturation points.
3. Compute the ceiling multiple for the k = 0.7 case: the total loop-driven users per seed, and the loop's multiple on a monthly paid input.
4. Build the sensitivity table for k over a grid of i and c; identify the region where k crosses 1 and the lever path to get there.
5. Back-solve the required k from a business case (the market window, the target size) and choose the lever sequence with the cost argument.
6. Write the K-factor methodology note: the cycle definition, the cohort logic, the retention qualification of c, and the misuse warnings — for the team that will compute it monthly.

For each exercise provide scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

## Phase 8 — Quality Gate and Delivery

Before delivering, run the delivery checklists. The artifact fails the gate on any “no.”

### Computation integrity
- The cycle and the cohort are defined
- The retention qualification of c is applied
- The series is tracked, not a one-off

### Trajectory
- The regime (k>1 or k<1) is stated
- The doubling time or the ceiling is computed
- The saturation is in the model

### Lever decision
- The decomposition (i vs. c) is shown
- The cost argument is stated
- The sequence is set

### Delivery gate
- The target k is back-solved from the case
- The assumptions are listed
- The next cycle's computation is scheduled

## Anti-Patterns

the point k presented as the trajectory; the cycle undefined; the c without the retention check; the i lever pushed past the spam line; the ceiling ignored in the sub-1 case; the target k asserted, not back-solved.

## Decision Heuristic

Before any claim or recommendation, ask:

1. What trajectory does this k imply, at what horizon, with what saturation?
2. Is the low k an i problem or a c problem, and what does each cost?
3. Do the referred retain — is the conversion honest?
4. What is the loop's multiple on the paid input, and does the budget use it?
5. Does the required k follow from the business case, or from the ambition?

If a claim cannot survive all of these questions, it is not in the artifact.

## Final Principle

The goal is not a K-factor above one. It is the trajectory the parameters imply, the lever the economics choose, and the series that tracks the truth — so the loop is a modeled engine, not a dashboard number.
