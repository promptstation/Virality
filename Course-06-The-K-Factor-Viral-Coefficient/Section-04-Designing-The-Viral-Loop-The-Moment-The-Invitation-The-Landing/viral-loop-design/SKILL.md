# Designing the Viral Loop: The Moment, the Invitation, the Landing: Professional Module Builder

## Mission

Produce professional-level material on how the loop is designed — the moment, the invitation, the landing — with the friction audit that prices every step and the quality gates that keep it honest. The unit of work is a defensible artifact — a learning module, an evidence brief, a case diagnosis, or a strategy memo — in which every claim is tied to a source, a mechanism, and a boundary condition.

Optimize simultaneously for:

- empirical precision (what the canonical studies and works actually found)
- conceptual clarity (the core terms kept strictly distinct)
- causal discipline (correlational and causal claims never blurred)
- practical utility (a practitioner can act on the output the same day)
- intellectual honesty (limits and boundary conditions as visible as the findings)

Do not produce generic advice or pop-science hype. The register is that of an advanced professional training program: confident where the evidence is strong, explicitly conditional where it is not.

## Use Cases

### New professional learning module
When asked to create a full module, course unit, or workshop on this topic:
1. Establish scope: audience, depth, and which claims the module must cover.
2. Build the evidence base from primary sources and canonical works.
3. Develop the core mechanisms and diagnostic logic.
4. Add method, critique, and application sections.
5. End with exercises and the delivery checklists.
6. Deliver the complete artifact with no placeholders.

### Brief or leadership memo
When asked for a short document that must decide something:
1. State the question the document must answer in one sentence.
2. Include only findings strong enough to bear that decision, each with its mechanism and source.
3. Convert results into plain-language implications.
4. End with the specific decisions the evidence changes.
5. Cap length; cut context before cutting caveats.

### Case diagnosis
When asked to explain why a specific piece of content, campaign, or product spread (or did not):
1. Identify the operative mechanisms — never a single assumed cause.
2. Map the case onto the diagnostic framework of this skill.
3. Run the differential: which mechanisms were active, which alternatives were considered and set aside.
4. State confidence and the evidence that would revise the diagnosis.

### Strategy design
When asked to design a campaign, content system, or program around this lever:
1. Fix the objective, audience, and platform context.
2. Select the mechanisms from this skill that fit that context.
3. Specify implementation, sequencing, and the measurement plan.
4. State the boundary conditions under which the plan breaks.

## Core Output Requirements

Regardless of format:

- Every named finding or claim carries its source or mechanism in the same breath.
- Correlational and causal claims are labeled as such wherever they matter.
- Effect claims carry their metric and a plain-language rendering.
- Boundary conditions are stated as explicit “applies when / breaks down when” conditions.
- Examples use real, named studies, campaigns, or artifacts — not invented case studies.
- No padding sections, generic intro/outro filler, or advice that would read the same for any topic.

## Phase 1 — Scope and Claim Audit

### Artifact and audience
What the user actually needs (module vs. memo vs. diagnosis) and who reads it. Different readers make different cuts of the same material.

### Claim inventory
List the claims the artifact will make. For each, note the source or mechanism that supports it and whether that support is correlational, experimental, or canonical. Downgrade or cut unsupported claims — not the caveat.

### Depth budget
Match length to the decision the artifact serves. The fix for a weak document is choosing stronger claims to carry it, not adding hedges to weaker ones.

## Phase 2 — Foundations and Core Content

1. Teach the moment design: the value moment (the user just gained — the file synced, the document created), the winning moment (the user just achieved — the goal hit, the insight found), and the handoff moment (the product's use naturally addresses another person — the shared document, the team invite) — the three moment types, and the evidence for which one converts in the category.
2. Teach the invitation design: the payload (the reason to open — the value preview, the personalization, the relationship line), the channel (the in-app, the email, the share sheet, the direct link), and the tone (the user's voice, not the brand's) — the invitation as the referrer's message, with the referrer's standing on the line.
3. Teach the landing design: the invitee's first screen (the value before the signup — the preview, the partial access, the social proof of who sent it), the path to the first value (the minimum steps, the moment the invitee gets what the invitation promised), and the exit (the graceful non-signup, the saved state, the re-invite path) — the landing as the promise kept.
4. Cover the loop architecture: the single loop (one moment, one path — the clarity case) and the multi-loop product (the loops per feature, the shared landing, the combined k as the sum of the loop contributions) — and the funnel placement (the loop at onboarding, at activation, at expansion).
5. Run the friction audit: every step from the value moment to the new user's first value, with its conversion rate and its cost — the funnel of the loop itself, and the step with the largest drop as the design target.

## Phase 3 — Method and Technique

- The moment inventory: the product's moments scored by value, win, and handoff type, with the conversion data where present
- The invitation spec: the payload template, the personalization fields, the channel matrix
- The landing spec: the first screen, the value-before-signup design, the path to the first value, the exit
- The friction audit: the loop's own funnel, the step conversions, the cost per step
- The architecture decision: the single vs. the multi-loop, the funnel placement, the combined k model

## Phase 4 — Analysis and Diagnosis

- Diagnose the loop's drop by element: the low i (the moment is not compelling, the share is not natural) versus the low c (the invitation is weak, the landing is cold) versus the mid-funnel leak (the friction step) — the element the audit isolates.
- Read the moment type mismatch: the loop firing on a value moment in a category where the handoff moment converts — the type, not the copy, is the fix.
- Check the landing's promise-keeping: the invitation's payload versus the landing's delivery — the gap is the breakage, and the bounce is its measurement.
- Detect the multi-loop interference: the loops competing for the same user's goodwill (the third invite in a week) — the frequency cap is the design, and the unfriend rate is its monitor.

## Phase 5 — Application and Design

- Choose the moment from the inventory and the category evidence; the moment is the loop's foundation, and the design work is the invitation and the landing around it.
- Design the invitation in the user's voice with the value preview; the personalization is the relationship, and the test is the open rate per referrer tier.
- Design the landing as the promise kept: the value before the signup, the path under the step budget, the exit that preserves the return.
- Run the friction audit before the launch and after each change: the step with the largest drop is the next design target, and the audit's cadence is the loop's standing work.

## Phase 6 — Ethics and Boundary Conditions

- The invitation is sent under the user's name; the line is the user's consent to the send (the explicit choice, the easy revoke) and the content's fidelity to what the user would say.
- The landing's social proof (the referrer's identity, the relationship) uses the relationship's visibility; the line is the invitee's and the referrer's consent to the display.
- The value-before-signup is an access decision: the partial access granted to the invitee is a product decision with a cost, and the line is the free-tier boundary, stated, not creeping.

## Phase 7 — Exercises and Assessment

For learning artifacts, include exercises that force the diagnosis skill, not recall. Use the standard set:

1. Inventory a product's moments: the value, win, and handoff types, with the conversion data and the recommendation for the loop's moment.
2. Write the invitation spec for the chosen moment: the payload template, the personalization, the channel matrix, and the open-rate test per referrer tier.
3. Design the landing: the first screen, the value-before-signup, the path to the first value under the step budget, and the exit.
4. Run the friction audit on a live loop: the loop's funnel, the step conversions, the largest drop, and the design target it implies.
5. Decide the architecture for a multi-feature product: the loops per feature, the shared landing, the combined k model, and the frequency cap.
6. Write the consent and fidelity spec: the send consent, the revoke, the display consent, and the free-tier boundary — as the loop's standing policy.

For each exercise provide scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

## Phase 8 — Quality Gate and Delivery

Before delivering, run the delivery checklists. The artifact fails the gate on any “no.”

### Moment choice
- The inventory was run
- The type matches the category evidence
- The conversion data supports the choice

### Invitation and landing
- The payload is in the user's voice
- The landing keeps the promise
- The path is under the step budget

### Friction
- The audit ran pre-launch
- The largest drop is named
- The audit cadence is set

### Delivery gate
- The consent and fidelity spec is live
- The frequency cap is set
- The unfriend rate is monitored

## Anti-Patterns

the share button as the loop; the brand's voice in the user's name; the landing that breaks the promise; the friction unpriced; the multi-loop without the cap; the consent as a checkbox, not a design.

## Decision Heuristic

Before any claim or recommendation, ask:

1. Which moment does this loop fire on, and does the category evidence support that type?
2. Does the invitation say what the user would say, and does the landing keep what it promised?
3. What is the largest friction drop, and is it the next design target?
4. How many invites can this user's goodwill absorb, and is the cap set?
5. Does the consent spec cover the send, the display, and the access?

If a claim cannot survive all of these questions, it is not in the artifact.

## Final Principle

The goal is not a loop that ships. It is a loop whose moment is the product's own, whose invitation is the user's voice, whose landing keeps the promise, and whose friction is priced — so the loop grows the product the way the product's value already grows it.
