# Step 2 — Generated Prompt (Result of the Reusable Prompt)

```
Develop a comprehensive, professional-level learning module on **“Practical Value: Content People Forward to Help Others”** as part of an advanced course on the science of why ideas, products, and messages spread socially. The module should how practical utility drives a distinct, close-network sharing behavior and how to design content that people forward to help others.

The material should teach the utility logic: content is forwarded when the sharer believes a specific person will benefit, and how to engineer that belief. Go beyond the assumption that emotional content is the only path to sharing and examine the evidence that usefulness predicts sharing and that it routes sharing to close ties.

State the utility finding: content perceived as practically useful is shared more, and the sharing goes disproportionately to close others — it is help, not self-presentation.

Teach the utility formats that carry transmission — how-to, templates, calculators, checklists, tools, annotated data — and why the specific and actionable beats the comprehensive and abstract.

Show the reader/forwarder test: the sharer must be able to name the specific person for whom this is useful at the moment of forwarding.

Teach the design of the forwarding frame: the share line that carries the recommendation ('this is for you because...'), and why utility content needs a forwarding affordance.

Diagnose the read-not-shared failure: content with value for the reader but no identifiable forward target, the common B2B content trap.

Include practical methods for working at a professional level:
* Auditing a content library for forward-utility (can a specific recipient be named?)
* Designing the utility format: how-to, template, calculator, checklist selection
* Measuring forwarding rate versus passive consumption rate
* Testing forwarding frames (recommendation copy) in A/B tests
* Building a utility-content calendar mapped to the audience's recurring problems

Include practical exercises that require the learner to:
1. Audit ten assets from a content library for forward-utility: name the forward target or flag the deficit.
2. Design a utility asset from a recurring audience problem: format, one-problem scope, forwarding affordance, and KPI.
3. Write five forwarding frames for the same asset and predict which will lift forwarding rate, then test two.
4. Build the utility calendar for one quarter mapped to the audience's problem seasonality.
5. Diagnose a high-read/low-share asset: forward target, format, or frame — and prescribe the fix.
6. Design the measurement: forwarding rate, network distance of shares, and the decision rule for doubling down on utility content.

For each exercise, provide the scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

Structure the material progressively, beginning with the utility finding and the close-tie routing, moving through utility formats, forwarding frames, and measurement, then to a utility-content program with forwarding as the KPI. Establish clear conceptual distinctions between **reader value, forward value, forward target, and forwarding affordance** before showing how they interact.

Use professional terminology from marketing science, social psychology, and communication research where relevant, but explain specialized terminology in clear language. Do not make the material sound like generic AI-generated advice; the register should be that of an advanced professional training module used by content strategists, brand marketers, and communication researchers.

Research the subject using high-quality professional and academic sources where external research materially strengthens the material. Prioritize authoritative sources: Jonah Berger's research program (Journal of Consumer Research, Journal of Marketing Research, and the book 'Contagious'), the word-of-mouth and social-contagion literature, and replication studies of the six levers. Avoid relying primarily on low-quality SEO articles, generic stepps framework blogs, or unsupported “best practice” claims. Where useful, distinguish established research findings from professional conventions and informed recommendations.

Pay particular attention to the relationship between this module and principles such as **close-tie forwarding, one-problem scope, forwarding affordance, and maintenance discipline**. Connect these concepts to concrete analytical and design decisions rather than discussing them only theoretically.

Create a professional framework that content strategists, brand marketers, and communication researchers could actually use in a real project. The final material should therefore function simultaneously as a learning resource, a practical reference, and a working methodology for content and communication strategy.

Throughout the module, prioritize language that is:

* Precise without being jargon-heavy
* Empirical without being statistical theater
* Confident about what the evidence establishes
* Explicit about what the evidence does not establish
* Conditional where the findings are conditional
* Practical where the decisions are practical

Where a claim depends on context (platform, audience, topic, measurement), explicitly explain the trade-off and the boundary condition rather than presenting it as an absolute rule.

End the module with **Forward-target test checklist**, **Forwarding affordance checklist**, **Utility design checklist**, and a **Delivery gate checklist** that a professional team can use before acting on content and communication strategy.

The final result should be comprehensive enough to serve as an advanced professional training module, but organized clearly enough that a learner can study it progressively and apply each concept in content and communication strategy.
```
