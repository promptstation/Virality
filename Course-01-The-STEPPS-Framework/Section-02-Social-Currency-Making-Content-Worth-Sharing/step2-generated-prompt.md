# Step 2 — Generated Prompt (Result of the Reusable Prompt)

```
Develop a comprehensive, professional-level learning module on **“Social Currency: Making Content Worth Sharing”** as part of an advanced course on the science of why ideas, products, and messages spread socially. The module should how conversational status — insider access, exclusivity, cleverness — determines whether content is worth sharing and how to design for it.

The material should teach the social-currency logic: content is shared when it makes the sharer look good, and how to engineer that deliberately. Go beyond the intuition that useful or important content is automatically shareable and examine the conversational-fit evidence and the status experiments showing sharers self-enhance.

Explain conversational fit: what a community talks about is what it shares, and currency is highest when content lands on the conversation people are already having.

Teach the four currency sources — insider access, exclusivity, cleverness, and aspiration — with the field evidence that people disproportionately share information that enhances their image.

Show how data can become currency: surprising internal numbers, benchmarks, and 'we measured this' content that lets the sharer appear informed.

Teach the sharer/audience distinction: content can be valuable to the reader and still fail if it confers nothing on the person who would forward it.

Diagnose the anti-currency failure: content that is humble, generic, or brand-announcing transmits poorly because the sharer gains nothing.

Include practical methods for working at a professional level:
* Auditing an audience's existing conversation (hashtags, groups, press) to find currency openings
* Scoring content for insider access, exclusivity, cleverness, and aspiration
* Converting proprietary data into shareable currency
* A/B testing status-framed versus neutral headlines for share rate
* Measuring share rate versus read rate to separate reader value from sharer value

Include practical exercises that require the learner to:
1. Write the currency profile of a piece of content that did not spread: which of the four sources it attempted, where it fell short, and the redesign.
2. Convert a brand's internal dataset into three shareable currency assets, each targeting a different source.
3. Map a community's active conversation for two weeks and propose five currency openings with fit justification.
4. Run a sharer test: interview five representative sharers about what their most-shared content says about them, and derive the currency rules for the brand.
5. A/B test four headlines for the same story — status, insider, cleverness, neutral — and interpret the share-rate differential.
6. Draft a one-page brief arguing that a product launch should lead with currency (insider access) rather than product features, with the evidence.

For each exercise, provide the scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

Structure the material progressively, beginning with conversational fit and the status motives for sharing, moving through the four currency sources and their measurement, then to deliberate currency design and sharer testing. Establish clear conceptual distinctions between **reader value, sharer value, conversational fit, and currency source** before showing how they interact.

Use professional terminology from marketing science, social psychology, and communication research where relevant, but explain specialized terminology in clear language. Do not make the material sound like generic AI-generated advice; the register should be that of an advanced professional training module used by content strategists, brand marketers, and communication researchers.

Research the subject using high-quality professional and academic sources where external research materially strengthens the material. Prioritize authoritative sources: Jonah Berger's research program (Journal of Consumer Research, Journal of Marketing Research, and the book 'Contagious'), the word-of-mouth and social-contagion literature, and replication studies of the six levers. Avoid relying primarily on low-quality SEO articles, generic stepps framework blogs, or unsupported “best practice” claims. Where useful, distinguish established research findings from professional conventions and informed recommendations.

Pay particular attention to the relationship between this module and principles such as **self-presentation, conversational fit, base-rate discipline, and scarcity honesty**. Connect these concepts to concrete analytical and design decisions rather than discussing them only theoretically.

Create a professional framework that content strategists, brand marketers, and communication researchers could actually use in a real project. The final material should therefore function simultaneously as a learning resource, a practical reference, and a working methodology for content and communication strategy.

Throughout the module, prioritize language that is:

* Precise without being jargon-heavy
* Empirical without being statistical theater
* Confident about what the evidence establishes
* Explicit about what the evidence does not establish
* Conditional where the findings are conditional
* Practical where the decisions are practical

Where a claim depends on context (platform, audience, topic, measurement), explicitly explain the trade-off and the boundary condition rather than presenting it as an absolute rule.

End the module with **Currency source decision checklist**, **Sharer test checklist**, **Data-to-currency audit checklist**, and a **Delivery gate checklist** that a professional team can use before acting on content and communication strategy.

The final result should be comprehensive enough to serve as an advanced professional training module, but organized clearly enough that a learner can study it progressively and apply each concept in content and communication strategy.
```
