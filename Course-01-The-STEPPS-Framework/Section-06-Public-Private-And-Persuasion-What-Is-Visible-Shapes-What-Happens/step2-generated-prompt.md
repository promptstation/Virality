# Step 2 — Generated Prompt (Result of the Reusable Prompt)

```
Develop a comprehensive, professional-level learning module on **“Public, Private, and Persuasion: What Is Visible Shapes What Happens”** as part of an advanced course on the science of why ideas, products, and messages spread socially. The module should how the visibility of behavior — public displays, defaults, ambient cues — changes uptake, and how to choose between loud and subtle influence.

The material should teach the visibility logic: what is seen is done more, and the design of public displays, defaults, and ambient cues — with the knowledge of when subtlety wins. Go beyond the assumption that persuasion is always a message problem and examine the visibility and default evidence, including when public pressure fails on identity-sensitive behavior.

Explain the visibility mechanism: observed behavior updates expectations and norms; making a behavior or product visible increases its uptake because the visible is the normal.

Teach the loud levers — public displays of use, visible defaults, ambient cues, open-by-default participation — with the field evidence that visibility shifts behavior.

Teach the subtle levers — private defaults, contextual prompts, unobtrusive architecture — and the evidence that subtlety outperforms visibility on identity-sensitive and low-stakes behavior.

Show the diagnostic: the behavior's observability (can others see it?) and its sensitivity (does performing it risk identity?) determine loud versus subtle.

Design the visibility architecture: where in the product, space, or message the behavior is seen, by whom, and at what frequency.

Include practical methods for working at a professional level:
* Mapping a behavior's observability and sensitivity
* Auditing a product or space for visible versus hidden cues of use
* Designing default states (open-by-default participation, visible usage)
* A/B testing public versus private versions of the same prompt
* Measuring uptake differentials attributable to visibility

Include practical exercises that require the learner to:
1. Map the observability and sensitivity of three behaviors and prescribe loud versus subtle influence for each, with the mechanism.
2. Audit a product for visibility: list the visible cues of use, the hidden ones, and the design change that would raise uptake most.
3. Design the default architecture for a new feature: open-by-default or not, with the consent and opt-out plan.
4. A/B test a public prompt versus a private prompt for the same behavior; interpret the uptake differential and the identity backfire check.
5. Diagnose an under-adopted behavior with a good product and a good message: visibility, default, or cue placement — and the fix.
6. Write the ethics review for a visibility campaign: what is observed, by whom, the consent plan, and the line that would make it monitoring.

For each exercise, provide the scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

Structure the material progressively, beginning with the visibility mechanism and the loud/subtle distinction, moving through designing displays, defaults, and cues, then to a visibility architecture matched to the behavior's sensitivity. Establish clear conceptual distinctions between **observability, sensitivity, loud influence, and subtle influence** before showing how they interact.

Use professional terminology from marketing science, social psychology, and communication research where relevant, but explain specialized terminology in clear language. Do not make the material sound like generic AI-generated advice; the register should be that of an advanced professional training module used by content strategists, brand marketers, and communication researchers.

Research the subject using high-quality professional and academic sources where external research materially strengthens the material. Prioritize authoritative sources: Jonah Berger's research program (Journal of Consumer Research, Journal of Marketing Research, and the book 'Contagious'), the word-of-mouth and social-contagion literature, and replication studies of the six levers. Avoid relying primarily on low-quality SEO articles, generic stepps framework blogs, or unsupported “best practice” claims. Where useful, distinguish established research findings from professional conventions and informed recommendations.

Pay particular attention to the relationship between this module and principles such as **norm updating, identity cost, default consent, and placement at the decision point**. Connect these concepts to concrete analytical and design decisions rather than discussing them only theoretically.

Create a professional framework that content strategists, brand marketers, and communication researchers could actually use in a real project. The final material should therefore function simultaneously as a learning resource, a practical reference, and a working methodology for content and communication strategy.

Throughout the module, prioritize language that is:

* Precise without being jargon-heavy
* Empirical without being statistical theater
* Confident about what the evidence establishes
* Explicit about what the evidence does not establish
* Conditional where the findings are conditional
* Practical where the decisions are practical

Where a claim depends on context (platform, audience, topic, measurement), explicitly explain the trade-off and the boundary condition rather than presenting it as an absolute rule.

End the module with **Behavior classification checklist**, **Visibility design checklist**, **Mechanism check checklist**, and a **Delivery gate checklist** that a professional team can use before acting on content and communication strategy.

The final result should be comprehensive enough to serve as an advanced professional training module, but organized clearly enough that a learner can study it progressively and apply each concept in content and communication strategy.
```
