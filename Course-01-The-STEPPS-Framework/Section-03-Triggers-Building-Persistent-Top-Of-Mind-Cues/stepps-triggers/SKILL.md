# Triggers: Building Persistent Top-of-Mind Cues: Professional Module Builder

## Mission

Produce professional-level material on how environmental cues create top-of-mind associations and how to build, measure, and sustain trigger strength over time. The unit of work is a defensible artifact — a learning module, an evidence brief, a case diagnosis, or a strategy memo — in which every claim is tied to a source, a mechanism, and a boundary condition.

Optimize simultaneously for:

- empirical precision (what the canonical studies and works actually found)
- conceptual clarity (the core terms kept strictly distinct)
- causal discipline (correlational and causal claims never blurred)
- practical utility (a practitioner can act on the output the same day)
- intellectual honesty (limits and boundary conditions as visible as the findings)

Do not produce generic advice or pop-science hype. The register is that of an advanced professional training program: confident where the evidence is strong, explicitly conditional where it is not.

## Use Cases

### New professional learning module
When asked to create a full module, course unit, or workshop on this topic:
1. Establish scope: audience, depth, and which claims the module must cover.
2. Build the evidence base from primary sources and canonical works.
3. Develop the core mechanisms and diagnostic logic.
4. Add method, critique, and application sections.
5. End with exercises and the delivery checklists.
6. Deliver the complete artifact with no placeholders.

### Brief or leadership memo
When asked for a short document that must decide something:
1. State the question the document must answer in one sentence.
2. Include only findings strong enough to bear that decision, each with its mechanism and source.
3. Convert results into plain-language implications.
4. End with the specific decisions the evidence changes.
5. Cap length; cut context before cutting caveats.

### Case diagnosis
When asked to explain why a specific piece of content, campaign, or product spread (or did not):
1. Identify the operative mechanisms — never a single assumed cause.
2. Map the case onto the diagnostic framework of this skill.
3. Run the differential: which mechanisms were active, which alternatives were considered and set aside.
4. State confidence and the evidence that would revise the diagnosis.

### Strategy design
When asked to design a campaign, content system, or program around this lever:
1. Fix the objective, audience, and platform context.
2. Select the mechanisms from this skill that fit that context.
3. Specify implementation, sequencing, and the measurement plan.
4. State the boundary conditions under which the plan breaks.

## Core Output Requirements

Regardless of format:

- Every named finding or claim carries its source or mechanism in the same breath.
- Correlational and causal claims are labeled as such wherever they matter.
- Effect claims carry their metric and a plain-language rendering.
- Boundary conditions are stated as explicit “applies when / breaks down when” conditions.
- Examples use real, named studies, campaigns, or artifacts — not invented case studies.
- No padding sections, generic intro/outro filler, or advice that would read the same for any topic.

## Phase 1 — Scope and Claim Audit

### Artifact and audience
What the user actually needs (module vs. memo vs. diagnosis) and who reads it. Different readers make different cuts of the same material.

### Claim inventory
List the claims the artifact will make. For each, note the source or mechanism that supports it and whether that support is correlational, experimental, or canonical. Downgrade or cut unsupported claims — not the caveat.

### Depth budget
Match length to the decision the artifact serves. The fix for a weak document is choosing stronger claims to carry it, not adding hedges to weaker ones.

## Phase 2 — Foundations and Core Content

1. Explain the trigger mechanism: an environmental cue activates an associated brand or idea, and the strength of the association — not the fame of the cue — determines whether the cue converts into action.
2. Teach how to find triggers: map the daily life of the category (meals, moods, places, routines, seasons) and list the cues that recur there.
3. Show how pairings are built: repeated, specific, and owned pairings of cue and brand, with the evidence that repeated co-occurrence in content and experience strengthens the association.
4. Teach trigger decay: associations weaken over time without reinforcement, and the content calendar must carry the trigger, not just the brand.
5. Distinguish persistent triggers (daily routines) from fleeting ones (holidays, news) and the different strategy each demands — the fleeting trigger is a campaign moment, the persistent one is a platform.

## Phase 3 — Method and Technique

- Mapping a category's daily-life cues (routines, moods, places, seasons)
- Auditing a content calendar for trigger ownership (does each asset carry the cue?)
- Building a cue–brand pairing scorecard
- Measuring mental availability with aided-recall and cued-usage surveys
- Tracking association decay over time in a longitudinal recall study

## Phase 4 — Analysis and Diagnosis

- Separate cue fame from association strength — a famous cue with no pairing does nothing.
- Diagnose forgetfulness between purchases as a trigger deficit, not a product deficit.
- Classify each candidate trigger as persistent or fleeting and match the strategy accordingly.
- Detect calendar drift: months where no asset carries the cue, and the recall dip that follows.

## Phase 5 — Application and Design

- Choose one persistent trigger to own, and commit the calendar to it.
- Write every brief with the cue in the first line — if the cue is not on the page, it is not owned.
- Design reinforcement cadence: the cue reappears at the rhythm the association needs.
- Layer fleeting triggers (news, seasons) onto the persistent platform rather than replacing it.

## Phase 6 — Ethics and Boundary Conditions

- Trigger engineering shades into reflex conditioning; the line is whether the cue serves the user's actual routine or merely interrupts it.
- Owning a cue associated with a harmful behavior (a trigger tied to overconsumption) inherits that harm.
- Frequency without substance trains indifference; the pairing must carry a reason, not just a presence.

## Phase 7 — Exercises and Assessment

For learning artifacts, include exercises that force the diagnosis skill, not recall. Use the standard set:

1. Map the daily life of a chosen category and produce its trigger inventory: persistent versus fleeting, each with the current owner.
2. Design the pairing strategy for one persistent trigger: the content cadence, the owned assets, and the reinforcement rhythm.
3. Build a cued-recall survey (10 questions) that measures association strength for a brand against two competitors.
4. Analyze 12 months of a brand's content calendar for trigger ownership and correlate the gaps with sales or recall data.
5. Take a product with weak between-purchase recall and produce the trigger prescription: cue, pairing, cadence, and measurement.
6. Draft the calendar for one quarter in which a fleeting trigger (a season) amplifies a persistent trigger (a daily routine), with the role of each asset.

For each exercise provide scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

## Phase 8 — Quality Gate and Delivery

Before delivering, run the delivery checklists. The artifact fails the gate on any “no.”

### Trigger selection
- The cue is persistent, not fleeting (or paired to a persistent one)
- The brand can own it credibly
- The user's routine genuinely contains the cue

### Pairing design
- Every planned asset carries the cue
- The cadence matches the decay rate
- The pairing is specific, not generic

### Measurement plan
- Cued-recall is measured at baseline
- Decay is tracked longitudinally
- The decision rule for reinforcement spend is stated

### Delivery gate
- Calendar ownership is visible in the briefs
- The decay schedule is on the calendar
- The next reinforcement is scheduled

## Anti-Patterns

famous-cue fallacy (the cue is known, the pairing is not); trigger without cadence; cue that interrupts rather than serves the routine; seasonal one-off campaigns mistaken for platforms; recall measured without cued prompts.

## Decision Heuristic

Before any claim or recommendation, ask:

1. What cue in the user's day could activate this brand, and how strong is the current association?
2. Is the cue persistent or fleeting, and does the strategy match?
3. Does every asset this quarter carry the cue?
4. What does the recall data say about decay, and when is the next reinforcement?
5. Would the user notice if the brand vanished from that cue for a month?

If a claim cannot survive all of these questions, it is not in the artifact.

## Final Principle

The goal is not more campaigns. It is a cue in the user's life that reliably thinks of the brand — built deliberately, measured honestly, and reinforced before it decays.
