# Step 2 — Generated Prompt (Result of the Reusable Prompt)

```
Develop a comprehensive, professional-level learning module on **“Triggers: Building Persistent Top-of-Mind Cues”** as part of an advanced course on the science of why ideas, products, and messages spread socially. The module should how environmental cues create top-of-mind associations and how to build, measure, and sustain trigger strength over time.

The material should teach the trigger mechanism — cue, association, decay — and the design of repeated pairings that make a brand mentally available. Go beyond the assumption that brand recall comes from frequency alone and examine the cue-exposure evidence and the decay data showing associations fade without reinforcement.

Explain the trigger mechanism: an environmental cue activates an associated brand or idea, and the strength of the association — not the fame of the cue — determines whether the cue converts into action.

Teach how to find triggers: map the daily life of the category (meals, moods, places, routines, seasons) and list the cues that recur there.

Show how pairings are built: repeated, specific, and owned pairings of cue and brand, with the evidence that repeated co-occurrence in content and experience strengthens the association.

Teach trigger decay: associations weaken over time without reinforcement, and the content calendar must carry the trigger, not just the brand.

Distinguish persistent triggers (daily routines) from fleeting ones (holidays, news) and the different strategy each demands — the fleeting trigger is a campaign moment, the persistent one is a platform.

Include practical methods for working at a professional level:
* Mapping a category's daily-life cues (routines, moods, places, seasons)
* Auditing a content calendar for trigger ownership (does each asset carry the cue?)
* Building a cue–brand pairing scorecard
* Measuring mental availability with aided-recall and cued-usage surveys
* Tracking association decay over time in a longitudinal recall study

Include practical exercises that require the learner to:
1. Map the daily life of a chosen category and produce its trigger inventory: persistent versus fleeting, each with the current owner.
2. Design the pairing strategy for one persistent trigger: the content cadence, the owned assets, and the reinforcement rhythm.
3. Build a cued-recall survey (10 questions) that measures association strength for a brand against two competitors.
4. Analyze 12 months of a brand's content calendar for trigger ownership and correlate the gaps with sales or recall data.
5. Take a product with weak between-purchase recall and produce the trigger prescription: cue, pairing, cadence, and measurement.
6. Draft the calendar for one quarter in which a fleeting trigger (a season) amplifies a persistent trigger (a daily routine), with the role of each asset.

For each exercise, provide the scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

Structure the material progressively, beginning with the cue–association mechanism and decay, moving through trigger discovery, pairing design, and measurement, then to a sustained trigger platform across the calendar. Establish clear conceptual distinctions between **cue, association, trigger strength, and trigger persistence** before showing how they interact.

Use professional terminology from marketing science, social psychology, and communication research where relevant, but explain specialized terminology in clear language. Do not make the material sound like generic AI-generated advice; the register should be that of an advanced professional training module used by content strategists, brand marketers, and communication researchers.

Research the subject using high-quality professional and academic sources where external research materially strengthens the material. Prioritize authoritative sources: Jonah Berger's research program (Journal of Consumer Research, Journal of Marketing Research, and the book 'Contagious'), the word-of-mouth and social-contagion literature, and replication studies of the six levers. Avoid relying primarily on low-quality SEO articles, generic stepps framework blogs, or unsupported “best practice” claims. Where useful, distinguish established research findings from professional conventions and informed recommendations.

Pay particular attention to the relationship between this module and principles such as **association strength, decay and reinforcement, persistent versus fleeting cues, and routine fit**. Connect these concepts to concrete analytical and design decisions rather than discussing them only theoretically.

Create a professional framework that content strategists, brand marketers, and communication researchers could actually use in a real project. The final material should therefore function simultaneously as a learning resource, a practical reference, and a working methodology for content and communication strategy.

Throughout the module, prioritize language that is:

* Precise without being jargon-heavy
* Empirical without being statistical theater
* Confident about what the evidence establishes
* Explicit about what the evidence does not establish
* Conditional where the findings are conditional
* Practical where the decisions are practical

Where a claim depends on context (platform, audience, topic, measurement), explicitly explain the trade-off and the boundary condition rather than presenting it as an absolute rule.

End the module with **Trigger selection checklist**, **Pairing design checklist**, **Measurement plan checklist**, and a **Delivery gate checklist** that a professional team can use before acting on content and communication strategy.

The final result should be comprehensive enough to serve as an advanced professional training module, but organized clearly enough that a learner can study it progressively and apply each concept in content and communication strategy.
```
