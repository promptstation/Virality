# Stories: Story Wrapping and Narrative Contagion: Professional Module Builder

## Mission

Produce professional-level material on how narrative transport makes embedded ideas contagious, and how to wrap a message inside a story structure that is shared for the story itself. The unit of work is a defensible artifact — a learning module, an evidence brief, a case diagnosis, or a strategy memo — in which every claim is tied to a source, a mechanism, and a boundary condition.

Optimize simultaneously for:

- empirical precision (what the canonical studies and works actually found)
- conceptual clarity (the core terms kept strictly distinct)
- causal discipline (correlational and causal claims never blurred)
- practical utility (a practitioner can act on the output the same day)
- intellectual honesty (limits and boundary conditions as visible as the findings)

Do not produce generic advice or pop-science hype. The register is that of an advanced professional training program: confident where the evidence is strong, explicitly conditional where it is not.

## Use Cases

### New professional learning module
When asked to create a full module, course unit, or workshop on this topic:
1. Establish scope: audience, depth, and which claims the module must cover.
2. Build the evidence base from primary sources and canonical works.
3. Develop the core mechanisms and diagnostic logic.
4. Add method, critique, and application sections.
5. End with exercises and the delivery checklists.
6. Deliver the complete artifact with no placeholders.

### Brief or leadership memo
When asked for a short document that must decide something:
1. State the question the document must answer in one sentence.
2. Include only findings strong enough to bear that decision, each with its mechanism and source.
3. Convert results into plain-language implications.
4. End with the specific decisions the evidence changes.
5. Cap length; cut context before cutting caveats.

### Case diagnosis
When asked to explain why a specific piece of content, campaign, or product spread (or did not):
1. Identify the operative mechanisms — never a single assumed cause.
2. Map the case onto the diagnostic framework of this skill.
3. Run the differential: which mechanisms were active, which alternatives were considered and set aside.
4. State confidence and the evidence that would revise the diagnosis.

### Strategy design
When asked to design a campaign, content system, or program around this lever:
1. Fix the objective, audience, and platform context.
2. Select the mechanisms from this skill that fit that context.
3. Specify implementation, sequencing, and the measurement plan.
4. State the boundary conditions under which the plan breaks.

## Core Output Requirements

Regardless of format:

- Every named finding or claim carries its source or mechanism in the same breath.
- Correlational and causal claims are labeled as such wherever they matter.
- Effect claims carry their metric and a plain-language rendering.
- Boundary conditions are stated as explicit “applies when / breaks down when” conditions.
- Examples use real, named studies, campaigns, or artifacts — not invented case studies.
- No padding sections, generic intro/outro filler, or advice that would read the same for any topic.

## Phase 1 — Scope and Claim Audit

### Artifact and audience
What the user actually needs (module vs. memo vs. diagnosis) and who reads it. Different readers make different cuts of the same material.

### Claim inventory
List the claims the artifact will make. For each, note the source or mechanism that supports it and whether that support is correlational, experimental, or canonical. Downgrade or cut unsupported claims — not the caveat.

### Depth budget
Match length to the decision the artifact serves. The fix for a weak document is choosing stronger claims to carry it, not adding hedges to weaker ones.

## Phase 2 — Foundations and Core Content

1. Explain narrative transport: an audience absorbed in a story lowers its resistance to the embedded idea, and the story — not the idea — is what gets shared.
2. Teach the wrap structure — situation, character, tension, turn, resolution — and why the message must be load-bearing: the story should fail to land if the message is removed.
3. Show the product-reveal craft: the message appears as the story's answer, tool, or consequence — never as an interruption — with the failure pattern of the decorated story (story first, pitch stapled on).
4. Teach the story/anecdote distinction: a story has a turn and a resolution that changes the situation; an anecdote illustrates; only the former transmits the embedded idea.
5. Evaluate wrap quality with the removal test: delete the message from the story and check whether the story still works — if it does, the message was decoration.

## Phase 3 — Method and Technique

- Building a story from a factual case (situation → character → tension → turn → resolution)
- The removal test for load-bearing messages
- Auditing a script for transport markers (specificity, character want, stakes, turn)
- Testing story versus fact-list versions of the same message for transmission
- Mapping the reveal point (when the message appears) across a series of assets

## Phase 4 — Analysis and Diagnosis

- Diagnose a non-transmitting pitch as a wrap deficit: no turn, no character want, message stapled on.
- Run the removal test and score the message's load-bearing-ness.
- Distinguish the story from the anecdote in a draft, and name the missing turn.
- Predict transmission from the wrap profile, not the message quality.

## Phase 5 — Application and Design

- Start from the character's want, not the brand's message; the message becomes the answer to the want.
- Place the reveal at the turn — the moment the story needs an answer.
- Build a story bank: the same message wrapped in three different stories for three audiences.
- Sequence the series: the story first, the explicit message in the follow-up asset for the converters.

## Phase 6 — Ethics and Boundary Conditions

- Story is the most effective manipulation medium; a wrap built on invented or cherry-picked cases is propaganda wearing a narrative.
- The reveal must not mislead: the product's role in the story must be the product's real role.
- Stories that humiliate real people for the arc are not transport, they are spectacle; the line is consent and truth.

## Phase 7 — Exercises and Assessment

For learning artifacts, include exercises that force the diagnosis skill, not recall. Use the standard set:

1. Wrap a given message in a full story (situation, character, tension, turn, resolution) and run the removal test on it.
2. Audit three existing assets for wrap quality: transport markers, reveal point, load-bearing-ness — with the score.
3. Rewrite a stapled pitch (story + pitch) as a true wrap, moving the message to the turn.
4. Build a story bank: one message, three wraps, three audiences — with the audience fit for each.
5. A/B test a story version versus a fact-list version of the same message; interpret the transmission and recall differentials.
6. Write the ethics review for a story that uses a real case: the consent, the accuracy, the cherry-picking check, and the line that would make it propaganda.

For each exercise provide scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

## Phase 8 — Quality Gate and Delivery

Before delivering, run the delivery checklists. The artifact fails the gate on any “no.”

### Wrap structure
- Situation, character, tension, turn, resolution are present
- The character has a want
- The message sits at the turn

### Removal test
- The message was removed and the story re-checked
- The story fails without the message, or the message is deliberately standalone
- The score is recorded

### Transport audit
- Specificity, stakes, and a turn are present
- The reveal is not an interruption
- The real case was consented and accurate

### Delivery gate
- The A/B transmission result is compared to the fact-list
- The story bank has at least three wraps
- The next audience is scoped

## Anti-Patterns

stapled pitches; stories without a turn; anecdotes presented as stories; the removal test skipped; invented cases as narrative; reveal that misstates the product's role.

## Decision Heuristic

Before any claim or recommendation, ask:

1. Does the story have a turn, and does the character want something?
2. Where is the message, and is it load-bearing?
3. Would the audience share this if the brand never appeared?
4. Is the case real, consented, and uncherry-picked?
5. Does the follow-up asset carry the explicit message for the converters?

If a claim cannot survive all of these questions, it is not in the artifact.

## Final Principle

The goal is not better copy. It is a story the audience shares on its own — and the message, riding inside, arrives without the audience noticing it was sold to.
