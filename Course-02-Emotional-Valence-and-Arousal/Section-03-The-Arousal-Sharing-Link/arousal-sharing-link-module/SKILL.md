# The Arousal–Sharing Link: Professional Module Builder

## Mission

Produce professional-level material on the central finding of the science of virality: that physiological arousal, not mere positivity or negativity, is the engine of social transmission. The unit of work is a defensible artifact — a learning module, an evidence brief, a case diagnosis, or a leadership memo — in which every empirical claim is tied to a design, a control set, and a boundary condition.

Optimize simultaneously for:

- empirical precision (what the studies actually found, at what effect size, in what sample)
- conceptual clarity (valence, arousal, discrete emotion, activation, and transmission kept strictly distinct)
- causal discipline (field evidence and experimental evidence never blurred)
- practical utility (a strategist or researcher can act on the output the same day)
- intellectual honesty (the boundary conditions and failures of the findings are as visible as the findings)

Do not produce pop-science hype. The register is that of an advanced professional training program: confident where the evidence is strong, explicitly conditional where it is not.

## Use Cases

### New professional learning module
When asked to create a full module, course unit, or workshop on emotional arousal and virality:
1. Establish scope: audience, depth, and which claims the module must cover versus which it may cite in passing.
2. Build the evidence base from primary sources (field study, experiments, replications).
3. Develop the causal chain and the mechanism layer.
4. Teach the valence-versus-arousal diagnosis as a standing analytical skill.
5. Add measurement, confounder control, and critique sections.
6. End with exercises and the four checklists.
7. Deliver the complete module with no placeholders.

### Evidence brief or leadership memo
When asked for a short document that decides something (a strategy change, a pitch, a board question):
1. State the question the document must answer in one sentence.
2. Include only findings strong enough to bear that decision, each with design and controls.
3. Convert effect sizes into plain-language implications.
4. End with the specific decisions the evidence changes.
5. Cap length; cut context before cutting caveats.

### Viral case diagnosis
When asked to explain why a specific piece of content spread (or did not) on emotional grounds:
1. Identify the emotion actually evoked by the content (evoked, not merely expressed or assumed).
2. Place it in the valence–arousal space.
3. Run the differential: arousal-driven, valence-driven, or neither (practical value, surprise, placement, network).
4. Weigh the alternative explanations with the same seriousness as the emotional one.
5. State the confidence level of the diagnosis and what evidence would change it.

### Replication or paper review
When asked to evaluate a study in this literature:
1. Extract design, sample, emotion coding, outcome definition, controls, and effect sizes.
2. Grade the causal claims against the design actually used.
3. Check the emotion coding method and its known limits.
4. Check the outcome denominators and selection effects.
5. Produce a structured review: what it establishes, what it cannot, and where it sits relative to the anchor studies.

## Core Output Requirements

Produce the artifact in the requested format (module, brief, memo, diagnosis, or review). Regardless of format:

- Every named finding carries its source, design, and scope in the same breath.
- The distinction between correlational field evidence and causal experimental evidence is visible at every point it matters.
- Valence effects and arousal effects are attributed separately; a result is never assigned to "emotion" when the evidence separates the two.
- Effect sizes are reported with their metric (rate ratio, odds ratio, coefficient) and a plain-language rendering.
- Boundary conditions are stated as explicit "applies when / breaks down when" conditions, tied to a study.
- No raw counts are presented without their denominator or selection context.
- Examples use real, named studies and real content patterns, not invented case studies.

Do not include padding sections, generic intro/outro filler, or advice that would read the same way for any topic.

## Phase 1 — Scope and Claim Audit

Before writing, determine:

### Artifact and audience
What the user actually needs (module vs. memo vs. diagnosis) and who reads it. A board memo and a researcher's module make different cuts of the same evidence.

### Claim inventory
List the empirical claims the artifact will make. For each, note the source that supports it and whether that source is correlational or causal. If a claim has no source that supports it at the strength being asserted, downgrade or cut the claim — not the caveat.

### Depth budget
Match length to the decision the artifact serves. A two-page memo that over-qualifies every sentence fails the reader as much as an under-qualified one; the fix is choosing stronger claims to carry the document, not adding hedges to weaker ones.

## Phase 2 — Empirical Foundations

Build the evidence base in this order, because each layer depends on the one before:

1. **The field anchor.** Berger and Milkman (2012, Journal of Marketing Research): nearly 7,000 New York Times articles; "most e-mailed" list and e-mail counts as transmission outcomes; eight emotions plus valence coded in the text; results — positive content more viral than negative; awe, anger, and anxiety (high arousal) positively linked to virality; sadness (deactivating) negatively linked; effects survive controls for surprise, interestingness, practical usefulness, and placement.
2. **The causal complement.** Berger (2011, Psychological Science) and the experimental pattern: manipulating the evoked emotion of a message (high-anger vs. low-anger, amusement-inducing vs. neutral advertising) shows activation mediates the emotion → transmission effect.
3. **Replication and extension.** Peer-reviewed replications in new settings (notably political communication on social media, where high-arousal messages raise retweet probability and negative valence lowers it), and note which replications use different platforms, outcome metrics, or coding methods.
4. **The theoretical frame.** The circumplex model of affect (valence × arousal) as the reason the two dimensions can be analyzed and manipulated separately.

Rules for this phase:
- Prefer the primary papers over secondary summaries; use secondary sources only to locate the primary ones.
- Record, for each study: design, N, coding method, outcome variable, controls, headline effect sizes, and the strongest realistic threat.
- Where replications disagree, preserve the disagreement; do not average it away.

## Phase 3 — Causal Chain and Mechanism

Develop the chain as the organizing skeleton of the material:

```
content features → evoked emotion (valence, arousal) → physiological activation
→ action readiness → sharing decision → observed transmission
```

Teach each link with its evidence and its failure modes:

- **Evoked vs. expressed emotion.** Content can display an emotion the receiver never feels; only evoked emotion transmits. Show how to tell the difference in a text or ad.
- **Emotion → activation.** The experimental mediation results: when activation is measured or controlled, the emotion effect on transmission shrinks or disappears.
- **Activation → sharing.** Arousal as a general readiness for action, with sharing as one discharge channel; this is why the effect is on transmission broadly, not on one platform.
- **Self-presentation and social currency.** Sharing positive high-arousal content enhances the sharer's image; this is the valence side of the story and it is real but secondary to activation.
- **Urgency and bonding.** Anger and anxiety mobilize to flag threats and injustices; awe shrinks the self and increases the drive to connect. Name the mechanism without collapsing them into "emotions make people share."

Keep the mechanism section diagnostic, not decorative: every mechanism must be stated so a reader could use it to predict an outcome in a new case.

## Phase 4 — The Valence–Arousal Diagnosis

This is the skill's signature analytical move. Teach and then apply:

- **Two negative emotions, different arousal** (anger vs. sadness): if the "negative content spreads" claim is really an arousal claim, anger should spread and sadness should not. The field data says it does.
- **Two positive emotions, different arousal** (awe vs. contentment): the same logic on the positive side.
- **Opposing directions.** In the political-communication replications, high arousal increased sharing while negative valence decreased it. The dimensions can pull against each other; a result must be decomposed, not read as a single "emotional" effect.
- **The differential.** For any case: arousal-driven, valence-driven, or neither (practical value, surprise, placement, network structure). A correct diagnosis names which alternative explanations were considered and why they were set aside.

Present the diagnosis as a repeatable procedure with its inputs (emotion coding of the content, sharing data with denominators, context features) and its outputs (attribution plus confidence plus what evidence would revise it).

## Phase 5 — Measurement and Evidence Critique

Cover the measurement layer with the same seriousness as the findings:

- **Sharing outcomes.** Raw counts, rates (shares per impression, e-mails per article), velocity. Explain why denominators and selection are part of the measurement, not a footnote: placement, article age, audience size, and platform mechanics all confound naive comparisons.
- **Effect metrics.** Rate ratios, odds ratios, coefficients — what each says in plain language, and when a "statistically significant" result is strategically irrelevant.
- **Emotion coding.** Lexicon-based, human-coded, and self-report methods; their agreement and their known limits (sarcasm, irony, culture, register).
- **Confounder control.** The standard control set from the anchor study (surprise, interestingness, practical usefulness, prominence) and the extended set for platform data (topic, timing, account size, format).
- **Correlational vs. causal.** What a field study can claim, what only an experiment can claim, and the exact sentence that overreaches in each direction.

End the phase with a worked example of a naive emotion–sharing correlation being corrected by confounder control, showing how the estimate moves.

## Phase 6 — Application and Design Implications

Turn the evidence into decisions without overreaching:

- Choosing a target emotion from brand, audience, and channel rather than from habit; high arousal is a tool with different costs per emotion (awe is brand-safe, anger is brand-risky).
- The stylistic levers that raise arousal — stakes, specificity, sensory detail, urgency, contradiction, novelty, personal relevance — each tied to a mechanism, not listed as incantations.
- When high arousal is the wrong tool: consideration-stage content, trust-building, low-stakes audiences, contexts where the cost of a false alarm is high.
- The negative-space guidance: the evidence supports arousal targeting; it does not support outrage farming, fear appeals, or manipulation as if they were the same strategy. State the line explicitly.

Every recommendation carries its evidence source and its condition of applicability.

## Phase 7 — Exercises and Assessment

For learning artifacts, include exercises that force the diagnosis skill, not recall. Use the standard set:

1. One-page evidence brief on the field anchor study (design, coding, outcome, controls, effect sizes, three realistic threats).
2. Code ten real headlines or posts for valence, arousal, and dominant emotion by lexicon and by human rating; quantify agreement.
3. Design a valence × arousal factorial experiment for a new format with cells, sharing measure, and analysis plan.
4. Differential diagnosis of a real "negative content went viral" case.
5. Interpret a replication's rate ratios in plain language for a marketing team.
6. Build the causal-chain diagram for a named viral post, evidence per link.
7. Write the rebuttal to "bad news travels fastest" with the conditions under which the original claim would be true.
8. Confounder-control table for a post dataset; show how corrections move a naive correlation.
9. Boundary-condition list ("applies when / breaks down when"), each tied to a study.
10. One-page leadership memo: what the evidence establishes, what it does not, and the three decisions that change.

For each exercise provide scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

## Phase 8 — Quality Gate and Delivery

Before delivering, run the four checklists. The artifact fails the gate on any "no."

### Primary-source evidence checklist
- Every named finding cites its study, design, and sample.
- Correlational and causal claims are labeled as such.
- Effect sizes carry their metric and a plain-language rendering.
- Replications and disagreements are represented, not smoothed over.

### Valence–arousal coding checklist
- Emotion attributed to the content is the evoked emotion, not an assumed one.
- Valence and arousal are recorded as separate values.
- The coding method is named and its limits stated.

### Confounder-control checklist
- Sharing outcomes have denominators, not just counts.
- Placement, age, audience size, and platform are accounted for or flagged.
- Surprise, interestingness, and practical value are controlled or considered.

### Arousal-versus-valence diagnosis checklist
- The primary attribution (arousal, valence, or neither) is stated.
- The two alternative hypotheses were explicitly considered.
- Boundary conditions are stated as "applies when / breaks down when."
- Confidence and the revising evidence are stated.

## Anti-Patterns

Avoid: the pop-science register ("viral emotions hack your brain"); universal laws where the finding is conditional; "bad news travels fastest" as an unexamined premise; raw sharing counts without denominators; collapsing valence and arousal into a single "emotion" variable; attributing a spread to emotion when practical value, surprise, or placement explain it; evoked emotion inferred from what the content displays rather than what the receiver feels; overclaiming causal language for correlational data and vice versa; effect sizes without metric; invented case studies presented as real; and advice that reads identically for any topic.

## Decision Heuristic

Before any claim about emotion and sharing, ask:

1. What design actually supports this claim (field, experiment, replication)?
2. What was controlled, and what confound still plausibly drives the effect?
3. Is this a valence effect, an arousal effect, or a mix — and can the evidence tell?
4. What is the effect size in its own metric, and does it matter for the decision?
5. Where is the boundary: which platforms, audiences, topics, or formats break the pattern?
6. Would a careful reader be able to reproduce every number from the cited sources?

If a claim cannot survive all six, it is not in the artifact.

## Final Principle

The goal is not to make the finding sound bigger. It is to make it usable: a strategist who finishes the material can diagnose any piece of content, a researcher can audit any study, and a leader can act on the evidence without inheriting its caveats invisibly. Precision, honesty about limits, and practical force are the same thing here — and the artifact succeeds when a subject-matter expert can trace every claim to a design, a control set, and a study.
