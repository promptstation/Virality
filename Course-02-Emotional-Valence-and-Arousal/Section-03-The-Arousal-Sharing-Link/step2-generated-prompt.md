# Step 2 — Generated Prompt (Result of the Reusable Prompt)

```
Develop a comprehensive, professional-level learning module on **“The Arousal–Sharing Link: The Central Findings”** as part of an advanced course on the science of virality and social transmission. The module should explain how emotional arousal — not merely the positivity or negativity of a message — drives whether content is shared, using the central empirical findings of the field as its anchor.

The material should teach how a piece of content evokes an emotion in the receiver, how that emotion carries a valence (pleasant or unpleasant) and an arousal level (activating or deactivating), and how the arousal it induces becomes a motivational force that increases the probability of social transmission. Go beyond the slogan “arousal makes things go viral” and examine the full causal chain: evoked emotion → physiological arousal and action readiness → the decision to share → observable transmission counts. Explain where valence still matters (positive content is, on average, more viral than negative), and why that average hides a more complex structure in which some negative emotions spread better than positive ones.

Explain the central field finding with precision. Reconstruct the Berger and Milkman (2012) study of nearly 7,000 New York Times articles: how “highly shared” was defined (the most e-mailed list and article e-mail counts), how emotion was coded in the articles (awe, amusement, anger, anxiety, contentment, disgust, sadness, fear, and valence), what the results showed (positive content more viral than negative; high-arousal awe, anger, and anxiety positively linked to virality; deactivating sadness negatively linked to virality), and what the authors controlled for (surprise, interestingness, practical usefulness, article placement, and external attention drivers). Teach the learner to state these results as conditional findings with their exact scope, not as universal laws.

Teach the causal-experimental complement. Explain Berger (2011) and the experimental pattern in which the specific emotion evoked by a message is manipulated (for example, high-anger versus low-anger versions of the same message, or amusement-inducing versus neutral versions of an advertising campaign), and how these experiments demonstrate that arousal mediates the effect of emotion on sharing intention and actual transmission. Teach how mediation logic works in this context: emotion changes activation, activation changes transmission, and the emotion effect disappears or shrinks when activation is controlled.

Explain how to distinguish arousal effects from valence effects when analyzing why content spreads. Show the diagnostic moves: compare two negative emotions that differ in arousal (anger versus sadness); compare two positive emotions that differ in arousal (awe versus contentment); read a result that is “more sharing for negative content” and ask which arousal channel is responsible. Use the political-communication replications (for example, party-leader messages on social media, where high-arousal messages increased retweet probability while negative valence decreased it) to show that the two dimensions operate partially independently and can even pull in opposite directions.

Teach the measurement of “sharing” itself. Cover raw counts, rates (shares per impression, e-mails per article), velocity, and selection: why placement, article age, audience size, and platform mechanics confound naive comparisons; what a rate ratio or effect size from the literature actually says; and the difference between correlational field evidence and causal experimental evidence, including what each can and cannot claim.

Include practical methods for working with the evidence at a professional level:

* Full-text emotion coding of an article or message corpus (lexicon-based and human-coded approaches)
* Valence–arousal rating protocols with inter-rater reliability
* Constructing the “most shared” outcome variable and its denominators
* Confounder control: surprise, interestingness, practical usefulness, prominence/placement, timing
* Rate ratios, odds ratios, and effect sizes for transmission outcomes
* Mediation analysis: emotion → arousal → transmission
* Reading and critiquing the original papers and their replications
* Building a minimal evidence brief from a primary source (design, sample, coding, controls, effect sizes, threats)

Include practical exercises that require the learner to:

1. Write a one-page evidence brief on the Berger and Milkman (2012) field study: design, sample, emotion coding, outcome definition, controls, headline effect sizes, and three realistic threats to the findings.
2. Code a sample of ten real headlines or short posts for valence, arousal, and the dominant discrete emotion, using both a lexicon tool and independent human ratings, and quantify the agreement between methods.
3. Design a factorial experiment (valence high/low × arousal high/low) that would test the arousal–sharing link for a new content format, specifying the message cells, the sharing measure, and the analysis plan.
4. Take a case where “negative content went viral” and produce a differential diagnosis: arousal-driven, valence-driven, or neither (practical value, surprise, placement).
5. Interpret a published replication (for example, a social-media political-communication study) and compute what its reported rate ratios mean in plain language for a marketing team.
6. Build the causal-chain diagram for a real viral post: the specific emotion evoked, the arousal level it carries, the action it makes likely, and the evidence that supports each link.
7. Draft a rebuttal to the claim “bad news travels fastest,” using the field and experimental evidence, and state the conditions under which the original claim would be true.
8. Design a confounder-control table for a dataset of published posts (placement, age, audience size, topic, platform) and show how each correction changes a naive emotion–sharing correlation.
9. Write the boundary conditions of the arousal–sharing link as a formal list of “this finding applies when… / breaks down when…” statements, each tied to a specific study.
10. Produce a professional summary memo (max one page) for a non-technical leadership audience: what the arousal–sharing evidence establishes, what it does not, and the three decisions a content team should change as a result.

For each exercise, provide the scenario, objective, constraints, expected deliverable, evaluation criteria, and an expert-quality example solution where appropriate.

Structure the material progressively, beginning with the emotion dimensions (valence, arousal) and the measurement of sharing, moving through the central field finding and its experimental confirmation, then to mechanism, critique, replication, and application. Establish clear conceptual distinctions between **valence, arousal, discrete emotion, activation, social transmission, and virality** before showing how they interact, and keep the distinction between correlational and causal evidence visible throughout.

Use professional terminology from social psychology, marketing science, affective science, and quantitative social research where relevant, but explain specialized terminology in clear language. Do not make the material sound like generic AI-generated advice or a popular-science article; the register should be that of an advanced professional training module used by content strategists, brand researchers, and social-media scientists.

Research the subject using high-quality professional and academic sources where external research materially strengthens the material. Prioritize authoritative sources: the primary Journal of Marketing Research paper and its replication literature, psychological-science experimental work on arousal and information transmission, the affective-science literature on the circumplex model of affect, and peer-reviewed replications in political and commercial communication. Avoid relying primarily on low-quality SEO articles, generic viral-marketing blogs, or unsupported “best practice” claims. Where useful, distinguish established research findings from professional conventions and informed recommendations.

Pay particular attention to the relationship between this module and principles such as **action readiness, self-presentation and social currency, the negativity bias and its limits, selection effects in measured sharing, measurement invariance of emotion across platforms, and the distinction between evoked and expressed emotion**. Connect these concepts to concrete analytical and design decisions rather than discussing them only theoretically.

Create a professional framework that a content strategist, brand researcher, or social-media scientist could actually use in a real project. The final material should therefore function simultaneously as a learning resource, a practical reference, and a working methodology for evaluating and designing emotionally strategic communication.

Throughout the module, prioritize language that is:

* Precise without being jargon-heavy
* Empirical without being statistical theater
* Confident about what the evidence establishes
* Explicit about what the evidence does not establish
* Conditional where the findings are conditional
* Practical where the decisions are practical

Where a claim depends on context (platform, audience, topic, measurement), explicitly explain the trade-off and the boundary condition rather than presenting it as an absolute rule.

End the module with a **primary-source evidence checklist**, a **valence–arousal coding checklist**, a **confounder-control checklist**, and a **arousal-versus-valence diagnosis checklist** that a professional team can use before acting on emotion-related virality claims or before designing emotion-targeted content.

The final result should be comprehensive enough to serve as an advanced professional training module, but organized clearly enough that a learner can study it progressively and apply each concept in real virality research and content-strategy work.
```
